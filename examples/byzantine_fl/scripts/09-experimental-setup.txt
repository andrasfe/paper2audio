Chapter nine. How the experiments were run.

Before the numbers, the apparatus. Three datasets, two models, two scales of federation, and four metrics.

The datasets are the standard image classification benchmarks. MNIST is the handwritten digit dataset: twenty eight by twenty eight greyscale images sorted into ten classes, zero through nine. FashionMNIST, from Zalando, is the same shape and size, twenty eight by twenty eight greyscale, ten classes, but the images are clothing articles rather than digits, and it is deliberately designed as a harder drop in replacement. Because the two have identical dimensions, they can be run through an identical model, which turns out to matter enormously for the interpretation of the results. CIFAR-10 is the natural image dataset: thirty two by thirty two colour images in ten classes, and genuinely harder than either.

Two model architectures. For MNIST and FashionMNIST, a fully connected network: seven hundred eighty four inputs, that is the flattened image, into a hidden layer of two hundred units with rectified linear activations, into ten outputs, for approximately one hundred fifty nine thousand parameters. For CIFAR-10, a compact convolutional network: two convolutional layers, three channels into four and then four into eight, with three by three kernels and max pooling, followed by two fully connected layers, five hundred twelve into thirty two into ten, for approximately seventeen thousand parameters. Note the inversion: the harder dataset gets the smaller model, because convolutions share weights. Hold that fact; it explains a result later.

The data is partitioned across clients using non identical splits, so different clients see different mixes of classes. This is what makes the problem realistic and hard, since honest clients genuinely disagree.

Now the two configurations. The small one uses fifteen clients: twelve honest and three Byzantine, so n is fifteen, f is three, and m, the number to select each round, is twelve. Training runs for thirty federated rounds. Local training uses stochastic gradient descent with a learning rate of 0.01 and weight decay of ten to the minus four, and gradients are clipped to a maximum norm of one, which is a standard stability measure and also means the crudest possible attack, sending an astronomically large vector, is off the table. The projection dimension k is one thousand. Distances for the QUBO are computed with cosine distance on those projections, and the problem is solved by the Neal simulated annealing sampler with one thousand reads. The classical baseline is given every advantage: it uses the same importance weighted projection and squared Euclidean distances on the projected gradients, with the number of nearest neighbours for Krum scoring set to n minus f minus two, which is ten. So the comparison is like for like; the only thing that differs is the decision rule.

The large configuration uses one hundred clients: eighty honest and twenty Byzantine, selecting eighty per round. Here the solver is a Leap hybrid sampler, a genuine quantum classical hybrid service rather than a classical simulation. Because both quantum time and classical time cost real money, this configuration runs only five federated rounds, on MNIST only, and only six of the thirteen attacks are tested: clustered, sign flip, Advanced Lie, Sparse Lie, Gaussian noise, and scale. Those six are chosen as representative, and they do cover both families. The classical baseline at this scale uses seventy eight nearest neighbours, again n minus f minus two.

Two caveats you should carry into the results. Five rounds is a small number, so the hundred client averages rest on a thin base; and the two scales are not directly comparable to each other, since they differ in solver, in round count, and in the fraction of attackers, twenty percent at the large scale versus twenty percent at the small scale, which is the one thing that does match.

Finally the metrics, defined in the paper's Table 2, all averaged over the thirty rounds. Think of each round as a classification problem in which every client is labelled Byzantine or honest by the aggregator. A true positive is a Byzantine client correctly rejected. A true negative is an honest client correctly retained. Detection accuracy is true positives plus true negatives, divided by n: the fraction of all clients classified correctly. F1 score is two times true positives, divided by the quantity two times true positives plus false positives plus false negatives: the harmonic style balance between catching attackers and not falsely accusing honest clients. Byzantine rejection rate is true positives divided by f, the fraction of actual attackers caught. Honest retention rate is true negatives divided by n minus f, the fraction of honest clients kept.

One consequence of these definitions is essential for reading the tables, so let me spell it out with the actual numbers. At fifteen clients, twelve honest and three Byzantine, the aggregator always selects exactly twelve and rejects exactly three. Suppose it catches none of the attackers. Then it has rejected three honest clients and kept all three attackers: zero true positives and nine true negatives, giving a detection accuracy of nine over fifteen, which is exactly 60 percent, and an F1 score of exactly zero. That is why the number 60.00 appears so often in the results tables with an F1 of 0.00 beside it. It is not a partial success. It is the floor: complete failure, dressed up by an accuracy metric that gets credit for the honest clients it could not avoid keeping. Whenever you see 60 percent accuracy with zero F1, read total failure, and whenever two methods differ only in accuracy while one has zero F1, the F1 column is telling you the true story.
