Welcome. This is a full length audio course, a little over an hour, built around a research paper titled Byzantine-Resilient Federated Learning via QUBO-Based Client Selection on Quantum Annealers, by Andras Ferenczi, Sutapa Samanta, Dagen Wang, and Jason Qizhe Qin, from American Express and Columbia University. I will assume you know essentially nothing about federated learning, nothing about quantum annealing, and only a little about machine learning in general. Everything the paper uses, I will build from scratch before it is needed: the mathematics read aloud in plain words, the attacks described one by one, the optimization machinery in detail, every table of results explained rather than recited, and the honest limitations at the end.

Here is the one paragraph version, so you know where we are going. When many parties train a shared machine learning model without pooling their data, some of those parties may lie. The standard defense, an algorithm called MultiKrum, scores each participant by how close its update sits to its nearest neighbours and keeps the ones that look most typical. That works beautifully against updates that stick out, and it fails against a cleverer adversary who crafts an update that is statistically unremarkable but still poisonous. This paper reformulates the defense as a global optimization problem, a QUBO, of the kind that quantum annealers are built to solve, and asks the machine to find not the individually most typical clients but the mutually closest group of clients. That change of viewpoint is worth roughly 8 to 10 percentage points of detection accuracy on exactly the attacks the classical method misses, and up to 97.78 percent detection on the hardest of them. It also costs accuracy on the easy attacks, which is the most interesting fact in the paper, because it means the two methods are complementary rather than competing. The authors then build an ensemble that decides, round by round, which of the two to trust, and that ensemble beats classical MultiKrum on every experiment they ran, averaging 96.73 percent detection accuracy against 92.07 percent at one hundred clients, with a 23 percentage point gain on the single nastiest attack.

A map of the journey, in four movements. First, foundations: what federated learning is and how a malicious participant attacks it, then the classical Krum and MultiKrum defense in full detail, and then the zoo of thirteen attacks used in the evaluation, which is where the story really starts, because the attacks split into two families that behave completely differently. Second, the machinery: a primer on QUBO problems and quantum annealing built from nothing, then the paper's own construction, step by step, from raw gradients to a cost matrix that an annealer can accept, and then the two scaling designs, the Cascaded Dual-QUBO and the MultiSignal ensemble. Third, the evidence: how the experiments were run, the results at 15 clients across three datasets, a chapter spent reading the round by round figures, and the results at 100 clients on real quantum annealing hardware in hybrid mode. Fourth, the reckoning: why the two approaches differ mechanically, what each of them costs in compute, what the authors could not test, and what they conclude.

Two housekeeping notes. Wherever the paper writes a formula, I will read it in words, and I will always say what it is for before I say what it contains, so you can let the symbols wash over you if you prefer. And wherever a number matters, I will give it exactly, because this is a paper whose whole argument lives in the gap between two percentages.
