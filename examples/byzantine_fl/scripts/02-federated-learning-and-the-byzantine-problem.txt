Chapter two. Federated learning, and why it can be attacked.

Start with the ordinary way of training a machine learning model. You collect all the data in one place, you run an optimizer over it, and you get a model. That is simple, and for many purposes it is unacceptable. A hospital cannot ship patient records to a technology company. A bank cannot pool its customers' transactions with another bank's. A phone manufacturer would rather not copy everybody's keyboard history to a central server. Federated learning is the arrangement that gets you a shared model without moving the data.

Here is the mechanism, and it is worth getting exactly right, because every attack in this paper lives inside it. There is a central server and there are n clients, sometimes called nodes or participants. The server holds the current global model, described by a big vector of numbers called the parameters, written W. In each round of training, indexed by t, the server broadcasts the current parameters, W sub t, to every client. Each client then trains on its own private data for a while, producing its own locally improved parameters, and computes the difference between what it ended up with and what it started with. That difference is the update, written G sub i for client i: the local parameters minus the global parameters. It is a vector with one number per model parameter, and for anything larger than a toy model that means hundreds of thousands or millions of numbers. The client sends that vector back. The server collects the updates from all n clients, combines them somehow, and produces the next global model, W sub t plus one. Then the round repeats.

Notice what the server has and does not have. It has n long vectors of numbers. It does not have the data those vectors came from, it cannot rerun any client's computation, and it cannot ask a client to prove that it trained honestly. All it can do is look at the geometry of the vectors it received and decide which ones to believe. That is the entire game.

The naive combination rule is the average: add up all the updates and divide by n. This is called federated averaging, and it is what the original 2017 paper by McMahan and colleagues proposed. It has a fatal property. The average is not robust. If one client sends a vector that is a thousand times larger than everybody else's, that single client dominates the average and effectively chooses the next model by itself. Even a single malicious participant can, in principle, steer the global model wherever it likes, or simply destroy it.

That failure mode is called a Byzantine fault, a term borrowed from a classic problem in distributed computing about generals who may be traitors. In this setting a Byzantine client is one that may send absolutely anything back: random noise, a scaled up copy of the honest direction, the negation of the honest direction, or a carefully engineered vector designed to look innocent. It is not assumed to be a crashed machine sending garbage; it is assumed to be an adversary who knows the defense and is trying to slip past it.

The formal setup used throughout this paper is: n clients in total, of which f are Byzantine, so n minus f are honest. The server's job each round is to select m clients to aggregate, where m is set equal to n minus f, and then average only the selected updates, scaled by a learning rate. If the selection is perfect, the Byzantine updates are discarded entirely and the global model advances as if the attackers were not there. If the selection is wrong, poison flows into the model.

That framing turns defense into a selection problem, which is why every method in this paper is a way of choosing a subset. And it tells you what to measure. If you know which clients are Byzantine, because you are the one who made them Byzantine in a controlled experiment, you can score the defense directly: how often did it correctly reject the attackers and correctly retain the honest clients. The paper reports detection accuracy, which is the fraction of all clients classified correctly, and F1 score, which weighs the catching of attackers against false accusations of honest clients. Notice that model accuracy is not the headline metric here. The paper is not primarily asking whether the trained model is good; it is asking whether the aggregation rule can tell friend from foe, round after round, which is the upstream question.

One more thing before we meet the defenses. In these experiments the honest clients do not all look alike either. Their data is split in a non-identical way, meaning different clients hold different distributions of examples, one holding mostly digits three and eight, another mostly ones and sevens. So the honest updates already disagree with one another. The defense has to distinguish malice from ordinary disagreement, and that is precisely the difficulty an attacker exploits.
