Chapter twelve. Results at one hundred clients: the ensemble pays off.

Now the second scale. One hundred clients, eighty honest, twenty Byzantine, selecting eighty each round, on MNIST, for five rounds, with the QUBO problems handed to a hybrid quantum annealing service rather than a classical simulator. Six attacks are tested, three from each family: clustered, sign flip, Gaussian noise and scale on the easy side, Advanced Lie and Sparse Lie on the hard side. Three methods are compared: classical MultiKrum, the Cascaded Dual-QUBO, and the MultiSignal ensemble. Tables 7 and 8 report detection accuracy and F1 score.

Start with what does not vary. On clustered, sign flip, Gaussian noise and scale, all three methods score 100 percent detection accuracy and 100 percent F1. Every one of them, perfectly, in every round. That is a meaningful result rather than a boring one, and it is a direct consequence of the ensemble's design: on those attacks the routing gate sees a large Krum score gap, classifies the round as outlier or magnitude, and hands the decision to classical MultiKrum, which is flawless there. The ensemble inherits the classical method's perfection on the easy family instead of destroying it, which is exactly what the plain QUBO method did at fifteen clients. Note also that sign flip, which classical MultiKrum failed completely at fifteen clients, at sixty percent accuracy and zero F1, is caught perfectly at one hundred clients by all three methods. With twenty attackers all sending the negated mean, the cluster of colluders is now large and far enough from the honest population that ordinary distance scoring finds it easily.

So the entire contest is on two attacks. Advanced Lie: classical MultiKrum gets 80.4 percent, the cascade gets 80.0 percent, and the MultiSignal ensemble gets 85.2 percent. Sparse Lie: classical gets 72.0 percent, the cascade gets 80.0 percent, and the ensemble gets 95.2 percent. Read those two rows again, because they contain the paper's headline. The cascade improved Sparse Lie by 8 points and moved Advanced Lie backwards by four tenths of a point, which is the failure the authors reported honestly in their methodology section. The ensemble improved Sparse Lie by 23.2 points over classical and 15.2 points over the cascade, and improved Advanced Lie by 4.8 points over classical.

Averaged over all six attacks, detection accuracy is 92.07 percent for classical MultiKrum, 93.33 percent for the cascade, and 96.73 percent for the MultiSignal ensemble: an improvement of 4.7 percentage points over the classical baseline, while, and this is the part worth emphasizing, preserving perfect detection on all the outlier producing attacks. If you are reading along, note that the abstract quotes this comparison as 95.3 percent against 91.8 percent, while Table 7 and the conclusion both give 96.73 against 92.07; the per attack rows and the 4.7 point gain are consistent, so it is the two summary figures in the abstract that sit slightly apart from the table.

Now Table 8, the F1 scores, where the story is considerably larger. On Sparse Lie, F1 goes from 30.0 percent for classical MultiKrum, to 50.0 for the cascade, to 88.0 for the ensemble. On Advanced Lie, from 51.0 to 50.0 to 63.0. The averages: 80.17 percent classical, 83.33 percent cascade, 91.83 percent for the ensemble. So a 4.7 point gain in accuracy corresponds to an 11.7 point gain in F1, and on Sparse Lie specifically, a 23 point accuracy gain corresponds to a 58 point F1 gain. Why the amplification? Because with eighty of one hundred clients honest, accuracy is dominated by the honest majority, which any method retains. F1 is computed only from the attacker related quantities, so it responds almost entirely to how many of the twenty attackers you actually caught. When the paper says the largest F1 gain is on Sparse Lie, from 30.0 to 88.0 percent, that is the number to quote, not the accuracy.

Why does the ensemble win so decisively on Sparse Lie in particular? The authors give the mechanism, and it is satisfying. Recall the attack: it modifies only the coordinates in the top five percent by variance, leaving the other ninety five percent exactly at the honest mean. So a Byzantine gradient under Sparse Lie is near identical to an honest one in ninety five percent of dimensions. That is fatal for a distance based score, and it is a gift to a rule that penalizes suspicious similarity. The suspicion threshold is the tenth percentile of the pairwise distances in that round, so it flags the closest ten percent of pairs, and near identical fabricated gradients land exactly in that bucket. The paper's own phrasing: this is exactly the pattern the threshold is designed to flag.

And on Advanced Lie, where the gain is a more modest 4.8 points, the credit goes elsewhere, to stage three rather than stage two. The authors write that the suspicion QUBO detects different subsets of Byzantine clients than classical MultiKrum, and combining both signals via QUBO primary voting yields a selection better than either method alone. That is worth pausing on, because it is a different claim from everything before it. It is not that one method is right and the other wrong; it is that the two methods make partially uncorrelated errors, and a voting rule that trusts the annealer as primary while still consulting the classical scores recovers clients that neither method would have found on its own.

Two limits on how much weight this section can carry. First, five rounds. The thirty round runs at fifteen clients showed classical MultiKrum decaying over time under Sparse Lie, collapsing to the floor around round eighteen; a five round experiment stops before that decay would appear, which if anything makes the classical baseline look better than it would over a longer run. Second, one dataset. Everything at this scale is MNIST with the fully connected model, so none of the architecture effects that made CIFAR-10 interesting can be observed. The paper is clear that the reason is cost: six attacks out of thirteen, to limit computational cost both on classical and quantum hardware. That is a fair constraint, honestly declared, and it is also the obvious place for a follow up study to push.
