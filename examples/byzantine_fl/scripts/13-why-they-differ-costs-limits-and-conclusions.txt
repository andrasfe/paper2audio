Chapter thirteen. Why the methods differ, what they cost, and what is left undone.

We have the numbers. Now the mechanism, which the paper sets out in its discussion, and which I think is the most durable part of the work, because it will outlive any particular hardware.

Classical MultiKrum evaluates each client independently, computing its Krum score and greedily taking the m clients with the lowest individual scores. The paper states the consequence in two sentences: any client whose gradients look normal will be accepted, and any outlier will be flagged as Byzantine. That is a complete characterization of the method. It is an outlier detector, nothing more and nothing less, and it will identify Gaussian noise, scale, blatant lie, same value, targeted, clustered, label flip and stealthy without fail.

QUBO based selection optimizes over all n choose m possible subsets using cosine distance, which makes it sensitive to the direction the gradient points, not its magnitude. The quadratic interaction terms encode pairwise relationships globally. As a result, the method catches updates that, although not outliers from a Krum score perspective, have their gradients pointing in the wrong direction. That is why it works on Lie, Advanced Lie, Sparse Lie and sign flip, all of which produce gradients that appear statistically similar to honest ones when evaluated individually.

Then comes the architecture argument, which explains the dataset differences. The convolutional network used for CIFAR-10 encodes learned spatial filter patterns, so a coherent gradient has a distinctive directional signature and the cosine signature changes are easily detectable. That is why the QUBO method performs so much better under the shuffle attack on CIFAR-10, 90.67 percent against 70.22, while the same attack is not identified as well when the model is simpler, at roughly seventeen thousand parameters for MNIST and FashionMNIST, since those do not create distinctive enough directional anomalies. A brief note on that sentence, if you are reading along: the parameter counts are the other way round in the experimental setup, where the fully connected model has about one hundred fifty nine thousand parameters and the convolutional model about seventeen thousand. The mechanism being argued, that structured convolutional filters give direction more meaning, does not depend on which count you attach to which model.

Advanced Lie gets its own explanation, and it is the cleanest statement of the paper's thesis. The attack modifies only twenty percent of coordinates with large deviations, leaving eighty percent unchanged; although it does not produce a significant Euclidean outlier, it shifts the gradient direction sufficiently to be detected by the QUBO formalism. Two metrics, two verdicts, one vector.

And then the admission I flagged in the primer, which I admire for being in the paper at all. It is a well known problem that annealing will perform sub optimally given a complex energy landscape. As a result, given scattered Byzantine gradients, the QUBO objective's global minimum itself likely includes Byzantine updates. In other words: on the attacks where the QUBO method fails, the failure is not that the annealer could not find the minimum. It is that the minimum was the wrong place to look. No better solver would have helped.

Now the costs, from the complexity analysis. Classical MultiKrum runs in order n squared times d, plus n log n, where n is the number of clients and d is the full gradient dimension. The n squared d term is the pairwise distances over full length gradients; the n log n is the sort. The QUBO pipeline runs in order n squared times k, plus gamma, where k is the projection dimension and gamma is the annealer time. Note the substitution: k instead of d. With k at one thousand and d at one hundred fifty nine thousand, the distance computation is more than a hundred times cheaper than the classical one, which is a point in the QUBO method's favour that is easy to miss. The cost that replaces it is gamma. For simulated annealing, gamma depends on the number of reads, one thousand here. For a real quantum annealer it includes embedding time and QPU time, and the paper notes it is bounded by hardware rather than input size. At fifteen clients the problem has fifteen binary variables and one hundred five quadratic terms, well within current hardware capability.

The two ensemble methods compute both: Euclidean Krum on full gradients, at order n squared d, and cosine Krum on projected gradients, at order n squared k, so their routing stage costs order n squared times the quantity d plus k. After that, the cascade pays order n squared d when the gap is above threshold and it simply uses classical MultiKrum, and order n squared k plus gamma when it escalates. The MultiSignal ensemble is the same shape: classical MultiKrum for outliers, and for the evasion route the dual distance matrix at order n squared k plus the QUBO construction and solve at order n squared k plus gamma. The conclusion the authors draw: the cascade and the MultiSignal ensemble incur no additional cost over the pure QUBO pipeline, in the big O sense. Which is true asymptotically, and worth qualifying practically, since computing both gap statistics every round means always paying the full gradient distance computation you were hoping to avoid.

Now the limitations, stated by the authors. Only three datasets were evaluated, and all the models are relatively small. Larger models may require larger projection dimensions, which may not be feasible given current quantum annealing hardware limits. And the sharpest one: the experiments were performed with each of the thirteen attack types in isolation, and the effects of a mixture of attacks have yet to be evaluated. In such a scenario the routing gate could become confused. Their example: if fifty percent of the attackers run Gaussian noise and the other fifty percent run Advanced Lie, the wrong regime could be selected. That is a real vulnerability rather than a hypothetical one, because a competent adversary who knows the defense would run precisely that mixture, using loud attackers to steer the gate into the classical branch while quiet ones slip through it. The proposed remedy is a paranoid mode, in which the union of the clients that both methods have opinions about would be flagged as Byzantine, that is, reject anything that either method distrusts, trading honest retention for safety.

I would add two limitations the paper does not list. The thresholds tau E, tau C, the suspicion percentile and the suspicion weight are all fixed constants chosen by experiment on these datasets, and a routing gate is only as good as its calibration. And the attacks here are static: none of them adapts to the defense it is facing, though the authors name this as future work.

The conclusion, in the authors' own accounting. At fifteen clients, the QUBO formulation achieves up to 97.78 percent detection accuracy on Advanced Lie on CIFAR-10 and 95.11 percent on MNIST, with an average 8 to 10 percentage point improvement on the five hardest evasion attacks across all three datasets. The MultiSignal ensemble scales to one hundred clients with 96.73 percent average detection accuracy, a 4.7 percentage point improvement, achieving Sparse Lie detection of 95.2 percent, up 23.2 points, and Advanced Lie of 85.2 percent, up 4.8 points. The suspicion penalized QUBO directly counteracts evasion attacks that exploit the standard QUBO's cluster seeking objective, yielding the largest F1 improvement, on Sparse Lie.

Future work, as they list it: scaling beyond five hundred clients using the hybrid solver's capacity; validating and hardening the method against mixtures of attacks; measuring genuine wall clock quantum advantage through a direct comparison on the quantum processor, which this paper does not claim; combining QUBO based selection with their earlier quantum embedding work into a single quantum pipeline; learning the routing function so that thresholds adapt to the observed gradient distribution rather than being fixed; and investigating adaptive attacks aimed specifically at the suspicion penalized formulation.

Let me close with what I think you should carry away, in three sentences. First: a Byzantine update can differ from an honest one in magnitude or in direction, these are independent kinds of evidence, and a defense that reads only one of them has a blind spot an adversary can aim at. Second: encoding the selection as a QUBO buys you a genuinely global, direction aware criterion, and the cost of that purchase is the loss of individual anomaly detection, which is not a bug in the solver but a property of the objective. Third: the practical answer is neither method alone but a cheap, measurable test for which regime you are in, and that test, the Krum score gap, requires no quantum hardware at all. The quantum annealer in this paper is doing real work, but the most transferable idea in it is knowing when to call for help.

That is the paper. Thank you for listening.
