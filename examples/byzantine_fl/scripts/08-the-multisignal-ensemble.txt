Chapter eight. The MultiSignal ensemble.

This is the paper's final method and its best performing one. It has three stages: a routing gate that decides what kind of round this is, a modified QUBO that is built specifically for the hardest kind, and a voting rule that combines the classical and quantum answers. I will take them in order, and then walk the whole thing as pseudocode.

Stage one, the dual feature routing gate. Compute two gaps rather than one. The first is the normalized Euclidean Krum score gap, delta E, computed on the full gradients, no projection. The second is the normalized cosine Krum score gap, delta C, computed on the importance weighted projected gradients. Both use the same formula as before: the smallest rejected score minus the largest selected score, divided by the standard deviation of the scores. So delta E answers, how cleanly does magnitude separate this round, and delta C answers, how cleanly does direction separate it.

Two cutoffs are defined, tau E for the Euclidean gap and tau C for the cosine gap, and the pair of gaps is sorted into one of four regimes. Outlier: both gaps are high, so both metrics see the attack clearly. Clustered: the Euclidean gap is low but the cosine gap is high, meaning magnitudes are ambiguous while directions separate cleanly. Magnitude: the Euclidean gap is high and the cosine gap is low, the mirror image. And evasion: both gaps are low, nothing separates cleanly, which is the alarm condition. For the first three regimes the paper's finding is simple: classical MultiKrum works well, so use it. Only the fourth regime escalates. The cutoffs were fixed by experiment at tau E equal to 0.2 and tau C equal to 0.5. And the authors add a nice piece of honesty: in our experiments we were unable to produce Outlier and Clustered scenarios; these are hypothetical scenarios for future proofing our system. In other words, two of the four boxes in the diagram were never exercised by real data. They exist because the taxonomy is complete, not because they were needed.

One detail if you are reading the paper alongside this: the conditions as printed use tau E as the cutoff on the cosine gap for the outlier and magnitude regimes, while the clustered regime uses tau C on the same quantity. Algorithm 1 matches the text, so it is deliberate as written, but it means the vertical dividing line in your mental picture sits at a different height on the left and the right. The accompanying figure draws it exactly as specified so you can see the shape.

Stage two, the suspicion penalized QUBO. This is the clever part. The standard objective encourages the selection of clients that sit close together. Against Advanced Lie, that instinct is precisely wrong, because, as the paper puts it, the attack produces gradients that are extremely close to the honest mean. Being unusually close to your neighbours is not evidence of honesty; past a point it is evidence of fabrication. Honest clients trained on genuinely different data are never near identical. Something that is near identical to the consensus, in a federation where nobody should be, has been manufactured.

So the modified objective discourages selecting pairs of clients whose gradients are suspiciously similar. Concretely: build the dual distance matrix on the importance weighted projected gradients, as in the cascade. Then compute a suspicion threshold, tau s, defined as the p-th percentile of the upper triangular entries of that matrix, with p equal to ten. Read that as: look at all the pairwise distances in this round, and call the closest ten percent of pairs suspicious. Notice this is adaptive, not absolute, so it retunes itself every round to the actual spread of the population. Then the quadratic coefficient becomes: minus two times the dual distance, as before, plus a suspicion weight w s times the larger of zero and the quantity tau s minus the dual distance. That second term is zero for any pair farther apart than the threshold and grows linearly as a pair gets closer than it, with the suspicion weight set to ten. As the paper says, for pairs closer than the threshold, the penalty term overpowers the incentive. A pair of updates that are nearly identical now costs a great deal to select together.

Stage three, agreement voting. In the evasion regime the method does not choose between classical and quantum; it runs both and reconciles them. Classical MultiKrum produces one selected set. The suspicion QUBO produces another. Every client therefore falls into one of four categories: agreed accept, when both methods selected it; agreed reject, when both methods rejected it; QUBO only, when the annealer selected it but the classical method did not; and classical only, for the reverse. Because we are in the evasion regime by definition, both Euclidean and cosine gaps are low, so the classical method's opinion is known to be unreliable and is given low confidence. A blended rank score is then computed, based on normalized cosine Krum scores, with a bonus for agreed accept and penalties for agreed reject and for classical only, and the m clients with the best blended scores are selected. The asymmetry is the point: QUBO only clients are not penalized, classical only clients are, because in this regime the annealer's directional evidence is the more trustworthy of the two.

Now Algorithm 1 in the paper, in full, which is really just the whole chapter compressed. You are given the number of clients to select, m, the two thresholds, and the projection dimension k. For each federated round: broadcast the current model and receive the gradients from all clients. Project every gradient down to k dimensions using the importance weighting. Compute the Euclidean gap and the cosine gap. If both gaps are above their cutoffs, this is the outlier case: run classical MultiKrum. If the Euclidean gap is low and the cosine gap is high, this is the clustered case: run classical MultiKrum. If the Euclidean gap is high and the cosine gap is low, this is the magnitude case: run classical MultiKrum. Otherwise this is evasion: run classical MultiKrum to get one candidate set, construct the suspicion QUBO from the dual distances, solve it by annealing to get a second candidate set, and merge them by agreement voting. In every branch, update the global model with the learning rate times the average of whichever set of gradients was selected. Then go to the next round.

Two things to appreciate about this design before we look at results. First, it is cheap by default. Three of the four branches never touch an annealer at all, so the expensive path is taken only when the cheap detector says the round is genuinely ambiguous, which is the right place to spend budget. Second, it is a fundamentally different kind of claim from the rest of the paper. Chapters five through seven argued that quantum optimization is a better decision rule. This chapter concedes that it is a better decision rule only sometimes, and spends its effort on knowing when. That is a more modest thesis and, judging by the numbers we are about to see, a considerably more successful one.
