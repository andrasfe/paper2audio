Chapter seven. Scaling up: the Cascaded Dual-QUBO.

The construction in the last chapter works well at fifteen clients. At one hundred it starts to come apart, and the paper says so plainly: for large client populations the solution above faces scaling issues, and there is a higher likelihood that Byzantine updates cluster tightly, thus confusing the optimization algorithm.

Two separate problems are hiding in that sentence, and it is worth pulling them apart. The first is computational. The number of couplings in the QUBO grows as the square of the number of clients: 105 couplings at fifteen clients, 4,950 at one hundred. Every one of those couplings is a physical connection the annealer must realize, and since real hardware wires each qubit to only a handful of neighbours, a dense all to all problem must be embedded by chaining physical qubits together into logical ones. The chains get long, the effective noise goes up, and the quality of the returned solution degrades. The second problem is statistical, and it is more interesting. With twenty attackers instead of three, the attackers are numerous enough to form a genuine cluster of their own. An objective that rewards a tightly grouped selection can be satisfied by the wrong group. As the number of Byzantine clients grows, so does the risk that the optimum of the objective you wrote down is not the answer you wanted.

The Cascaded Dual-QUBO is the authors' first response, and its logic is: do not use the expensive machinery unless you need it. So you need a cheap test for whether you need it, and the test they build is the Krum score gap.

Here is the statistic, Equation 13. Run classical Krum scoring, producing one score per client. Sort them, and split them into the set the method would select, and the set it would reject. Now take the smallest score among the rejected clients, and subtract the largest score among the selected clients. That difference is the margin between the two groups: how big a gulf separates the worst client you kept from the best client you threw away. Divide by the standard deviation of all the scores, to make the number dimensionless and comparable across rounds and datasets. That normalized margin is delta, the Krum score gap.

The interpretation is entirely intuitive. A large delta means the classifier had an easy call: there is clear daylight between the accepted and rejected groups, and any reasonable method would have drawn the line in the same place. A small delta means the decision was a coin toss, with the last accepted and the first rejected client separated by less than the noise in the scores. And a small gap is the signature of exactly the attacks we care about, since an attack that succeeds by looking normal is an attack that leaves no daylight.

So the cascade sets a gap threshold, tau. If the Euclidean Krum score gap is above the threshold, the round is easy: use classical MultiKrum, pay nothing, move on. If the gap is below the threshold, the classical method is, in the paper's phrasing, not good enough, and we escalate.

The escalation is the dual part. Rather than building the QUBO on one notion of distance, the paper blends the two. Equation 14 defines the dual distance as alpha times the normalized Euclidean distance, plus one minus alpha times the cosine distance, with alpha set to one half. An equal blend. So each entry of the cost matrix now carries both signals at once: how far apart two updates are in magnitude, and how differently they point. The Euclidean part has to be normalized first, which is what that division by the largest entry was for, since raw Euclidean distances can be any size while cosine distances live between zero and two, and blending unnormalized quantities would let one term silently dominate. The resulting matrix goes through the same machinery as before, the Lagrange penalty and the coefficient formulas, and the resulting problem is handed to a hybrid quantum annealing solver rather than a purely classical one.

And now the honest part, which the paper puts in its own methodology section rather than burying: our initial Cascaded Dual-QUBO approach for 100 client instances, however, did not work for Advanced Lie. When we get to the numbers you will see exactly what that means. On Sparse Lie the cascade did help, moving detection accuracy from 72 percent up to 80 percent. On Advanced Lie it moved it from 80.4 percent down to 80.0 percent, which is to say, nowhere. Averaged over the six attacks tested at that scale, the cascade earned 93.33 percent against classical MultiKrum's 92.07 percent: real, but thin.

Why did it stall? The blend is the problem. Averaging a magnitude signal and a direction signal with equal weight is only the right thing to do when both signals are informative. Under Advanced Lie the Euclidean half of the blend is not merely uninformative, it is actively misleading, because the attack was designed to look ordinary in exactly that metric. Mixing a good signal with a deceptive one at equal strength gives you a diluted signal. That diagnosis is what motivates the next design, where instead of averaging the two signals, the method uses them to decide which regime it is in, and then applies a targeted instrument. That is the MultiSignal ensemble, and it is the subject of the next chapter.
