Chapter six. Building the QUBO: from raw gradients to a cost matrix.

Now the construction at the heart of the paper. The goal is stated in one line: replace the greedy MultiKrum algorithm with a method that holistically selects the best subset. Three steps get us there.

Step one, dimension reduction. The gradient vectors are enormous. Even the small models in these experiments have between seventeen thousand and one hundred fifty nine thousand parameters, and real deployments have millions. Computing pairwise distances in that space is expensive, and it is also noisy, because most coordinates carry almost no information about who is lying. So the first move is to project each gradient into a much smaller space of dimension k, and the projection used here is not random, it is importance weighted. For each coordinate j, compute the variance of that coordinate across all clients: how much do the clients disagree about this particular parameter. Sort the coordinates by that variance and keep the top k. The intuition is direct: coordinates where every client agrees carry no discriminative signal at all, since honest and Byzantine updates look identical there; the coordinates where clients disagree are where the evidence lives. Call the resulting reduced vector for client i, p sub i, living in k dimensions. In the experiments, k is set to one thousand.

There is a subtlety worth naming, and the authors exploit it later. Selecting coordinates by variance is itself a decision that can be gamed, and it also happens to be exactly where the Sparse Lie attack strikes, since that attack perturbs the top five percent of coordinates by variance. So the projection is aimed at the same coordinates the cleverest attacker aims at. That is either a happy coincidence or a design insight, and in the ensemble later it becomes a design insight.

Step two, the distance matrix. From the projected gradients we build an n by n matrix D whose entry i j says how dissimilar clients i and j are. Two different notions of dissimilarity are used, and the difference between them is the intellectual core of the paper.

The first is cosine distance. Take the dot product of p i and p j, divide by the product of their lengths, and subtract the result from one. A small epsilon, ten to the minus eight, is added to the denominator to prevent division by zero. What this measures is the angle between two vectors and nothing else. Two updates that point the same way have cosine distance zero, no matter how long they are; two updates at right angles have distance one; two that point in opposite directions have distance two. Because it ignores magnitude entirely, cosine distance is, in the paper's words, well suited to detecting attacks that preserve gradient norms but alter directions. That is the evasion family from the last chapter, exactly.

The second is Euclidean distance, the ordinary length of the difference between two projected vectors. This captures magnitude dissimilarity, and it is what classical MultiKrum uses. For the later dual formulation, the Euclidean matrix is normalized to the range zero to one by dividing every entry by the largest entry in the matrix, so that it can be blended with cosine distances on a common scale.

Step three, turning distances into a QUBO. Let x sub i be one if client i is selected. We want to select m equals n minus f clients. The paper's objective, as printed in Equation 8, is: minimize the sum over i of the row total of D for client i, times x sub i, minus two times the sum over pairs i less than j of D i j times x i times x j.

A note for careful listeners, because the algebra here rewards a moment's attention. If you expand that expression, the first term counts, for every selected client, its distance to all clients, and the second term subtracts twice the distances among selected pairs. What survives is precisely the total distance between the selected group and the rejected group. Equivalently, given the sign on the quadratic term, which is minus two D i j, putting two distant clients into the selected set together lowers the cost. The stated goal, finding the mutually closest group of m clients, corresponds to the opposite sign on that quadratic term, that is, to minimizing the internal pairwise distance among the selected. I mention it because the formulation as printed is internally consistent, Equations 10 and 11 follow from it exactly, and it is the kind of sign convention a reader should check against the implementation before reusing the formulas. It does not change the empirical results reported later, which come from the authors' code.

Whatever the sign, we now need the cardinality requirement, and here is where unconstrained bites. You want exactly m clients selected, but a QUBO cannot carry a side condition. So you add a penalty term: lambda times the square of, the sum of all the x variables minus m. If exactly m variables are on, that square is zero and you pay nothing. If you select one too many or one too few, you pay lambda. Two too many, you pay four lambda, because the penalty is quadratic. How big should lambda be? Large enough that violating the constraint always costs more than any saving you could get from the objective. The paper sets it to ten times the largest entry of the distance matrix, which comfortably dominates.

Now watch the penalty fold into the same matrix form, which is the standard trick and worth seeing once. Expand the square: you get the sum of x i squared, plus twice the sum over pairs of x i x j, minus two m times the sum of x i, plus m squared. Two simplifications apply. Because the variables are binary, x i squared equals x i, so that first term is linear, not quadratic. And m squared is a constant, which cannot affect which assignment is best, so it is discarded. Collecting terms gives the final QUBO coefficients. The diagonal entry Q i i is the row total of D for client i, plus lambda times the quantity one minus two m. The off diagonal entry Q i j, for i less than j, is minus two D i j, plus two lambda. That is the whole matrix. Two lines of algebra, and the selection problem is now in the exact form a quantum annealer accepts.

Then you solve it. For the small instances, the paper uses the Neal simulated annealing sampler with one thousand reads, meaning a thousand independent annealing runs, and takes the best energy found. The solver returns a set S of selected client indices. Because the cardinality constraint is soft, the answer can come back with the wrong number of clients, so there is a post processing step: if the size of S is not m, clients are added or removed based on their distance sums until it is. Finally the global model is updated: the new parameters equal the old parameters plus a learning rate eta, times the average of the selected clients' gradients. That is Equation 12, and it is the same aggregation step MultiKrum performs, differing only in who made the guest list.

Step back and see what changed. Classical MultiKrum computes n independent scores and sorts them. This procedure searches over subsets, all n choose m of them, with the pairwise structure encoded directly in the couplings. For 15 clients choosing 12, that is 455 subsets, trivial. For 100 clients choosing 80, it is a number with more than twenty digits, and exhaustive search is out of the question, which is the paper's stated motivation for reaching for an annealer at all.
