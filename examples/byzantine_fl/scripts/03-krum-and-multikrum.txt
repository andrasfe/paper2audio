Chapter three. Krum, MultiKrum, and the blind spot they share.

The defense everyone reaches for first was published in 2017 by Blanchard, El Mhamdi, Guerraoui and Stainer, and it is called Krum. It is built on a single idea: a Byzantine update, whatever else it is, tends to be far away from the crowd, so measure distance to the crowd and keep whoever is closest.

Here is the construction exactly. You have n gradient vectors, G one through G n, each living in a space of d dimensions, where d is the number of model parameters. The distance between two of them is the ordinary straight line distance: take the difference of the two vectors, and take its length. In symbols, D of G i and G j is the norm of G i minus G j. That is Euclidean distance, the thing Pythagoras would recognize, generalized to a million dimensions.

Now the scoring. For each client i, look at all the other clients, compute the distance from i to each of them, sort those distances, and keep the closest n minus f minus 2 of them. Call that set of neighbours the neighbourhood of i. The Krum score of client i is the sum of the squared distances to just those nearest neighbours. Low score means you sit inside a dense cluster of updates that resemble yours. High score means you are out on your own. Krum then selects the single client with the lowest score and uses its update, and only its update, as the aggregate.

Two design choices deserve comment. First, why the sum over only the nearest n minus f minus 2 neighbours, rather than over everyone? Because if you summed over everyone, the Byzantine clients would drag every honest client's score upward, and if the attackers colluded and sat near each other, they could distort the ranking. By truncating to the nearest neighbours you make the score depend only on the local density around each client, which the attackers cannot manipulate from a distance. The specific count, n minus f minus 2, is the largest number of neighbours you can use while still guaranteeing that at least a majority of them are honest, given that up to f clients may lie. Second, why squared distances? Partly for mathematical convenience in the original proofs, and partly because squaring amplifies the penalty for being far away, which is exactly what you want if far away means suspicious.

Krum keeps one client per round, which throws away almost all the honest work. MultiKrum, from El Mhamdi and colleagues, fixes that with the obvious generalization: compute the Krum score for every client, sort the scores, take the m clients with the lowest scores, and average their updates. That set is written as the top m, in the sense of the m best scores, and the aggregate is one over m times the sum of the selected gradients. In this paper, m is n minus f, so MultiKrum tries to keep exactly the honest population.

Now the blind spot, which is the reason this paper exists. Look carefully at what MultiKrum computes: for each client, one number, computed from that client's own neighbourhood, in isolation. Then it sorts those numbers and takes the best m. It never asks whether the m clients it selected are close to one another. It is a greedy, per-client procedure, and greedy procedures can be led astray. The paper states the consequence in a single sentence: MultiKrum does not optimize over subsets jointly, so any single Byzantine gradient that is close to its neighbours will evade detection.

Think about what that gives an attacker. You do not need to be close to the honest mean. You only need to be closer to your own neighbours than the least typical honest client is to hers. In a non-identically distributed federation, the honest clients are genuinely spread out, and some honest client is always the odd one out. An attacker who places a poisoned update in a modestly dense region will beat that honest client on the score and take her place in the selected set. Worse, if several attackers coordinate and place themselves near each other, they form their own dense cluster, and each of them enjoys a low Krum score computed from the others. The score cannot tell a tight cluster of colluders from a tight cluster of honest agreement.

That is the exact opening that the attack family called Lie exploits, from a 2019 paper by Baruch, Baruch and Goldberg with the memorable title A Little Is Enough. Their observation is that the perturbation needed to damage a model is much smaller than the perturbation needed to look anomalous, so you can sit safely inside the honest cloud and still poison the average. We will meet that attack and its descendants in the next chapter.

Before we leave the classical world, a quick survey of the neighbourhood, because the paper's related work section is a useful map. Some methods aggregate coordinate by coordinate rather than client by client, taking the median or a trimmed mean of each parameter across clients, which is robust but blind to correlations between parameters. DRACO uses redundant computation, having clients compute overlapping work so the server can cross check. FLAME clusters updates by cosine similarity to defend against backdoors. Sentinel combines cosine filtering with bootstrap loss validation. CosDefense flags outliers by directional similarity. Dim-Krum applies the Krum idea dimension by dimension for language tasks. And on the quantum side there is a small literature: Bender's decomposition and quantum annealing applied to scheduling in federated networks, a quantum Byzantine agreement protocol for blockchain based federated learning, quantum key distribution used to protect the updates in transit, and hybrid quantum classical models tested for robustness. The authors' own prior work took a different quantum route, using parameterized quantum circuits to embed projected gradients into a Hilbert space and detecting attackers with a quantum kernel. The paper you are listening to stays in the optimization paradigm instead, and the distinction matters: the earlier work changed the representation, this one changes the decision rule. What every one of these classical methods shares, and what the authors highlight, is that they all compute something local, per client or per dimension, rather than optimizing over the whole subset at once.
