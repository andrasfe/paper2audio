Chapter eleven. Reading the round by round figures.

The paper contains six figures, and they all have the same form: detection accuracy on the vertical axis, from zero to one hundred percent; the federated round on the horizontal axis, from one to thirty; a blue line for classical MultiKrum; an orange line for the QUBO method solved by simulated annealing. Two attacks, Advanced Lie and Sparse Lie, across three datasets. If you are in the player, they appear beside this chapter one at a time and each has its own spoken description. Here I want to talk about what this kind of plot shows that a table cannot.

An average over thirty rounds compresses a process into a point. These traces let you see the process. And the first thing you notice is that both lines are step functions, not smooth curves. That is a consequence of the arithmetic from chapter nine: with fifteen clients, three of them Byzantine, and exactly twelve selected, the only possible per round accuracies are 60 percent for zero attackers caught, 73.33 percent for one, 86.67 percent for two, and 100 percent for all three. Every point on every one of these curves is one of those four values. So the vertical position of a line at any round is simply how many of the three attackers were caught that round, and the line's shape tells you about consistency.

Figure 1, MNIST under Advanced Lie, is the archetype. The blue line is a sawtooth. It touches 100 percent at a handful of rounds, sits at 73.33 or 86.67 most of the time, and drops to 60 percent twice. It has no trend; it is oscillating essentially at random. The orange line spends the majority of rounds pinned at 100 percent, dipping to 86.67 and once, early, to 73.33. Same problem, same data, and one method is reliable while the other is guessing. That difference in variance matters operationally more than the difference in means, because a defense that catches all attackers in half the rounds and none in the other half lets poison into the model on a regular schedule.

Figure 2, MNIST under Sparse Lie, tells a completely different story with the same ingredients, and it is my favourite plot in the paper. The blue line starts well, at 86.67 percent for the first two rounds, then decays: down to 73.33, a dip to 60, back up briefly, and from round eighteen onward it is flat at 60 percent for the remaining thirteen rounds. Sixty percent, from chapter nine, means zero attackers caught. Classical MultiKrum does not merely underperform against Sparse Lie; it collapses, permanently, partway through training. Meanwhile the orange line is flat at 73.33 percent for the first fifteen rounds and then steps up to 86.67 percent and stays there for the rest of the run. It never reaches 100, so it is never catching all three, but it improves and then holds.

Two lessons from that pair of shapes. First, the decay of the blue line is a feedback effect: once poisoned updates enter the model, the honest gradients start responding to a corrupted model, the population drifts, and the attacker's job gets easier every round. Failures in this setting compound. Second, the step in the orange line, and the fact that both lines are so nearly flat, tell you the QUBO method's behaviour is deterministic in character. It is not oscillating with the luck of a particular round; it is making the same structural decision every time, catching the same two of three attackers.

Figures 3 and 4 are CIFAR-10. Figure 3, under Advanced Lie, shows the widest visual gap in the paper: the orange line sits at 100 percent for most of the run with occasional dips, the blue line thrashes between 60 and 100. Those are the 97.78 against 75.56 numbers from Table 4, drawn. Figure 4, Sparse Lie on CIFAR-10, is the counter example that keeps the paper honest. The blue line is perfect, 100 percent, for the first seven rounds, and spikes back to 100 at rounds nine, fourteen and fifteen, before settling at 60 for the rest. The orange line is a perfectly straight horizontal line at 73.33 percent for all thirty rounds. The averages are 72.44 against 73.33, which reads in the table as a near tie, and the plot shows that the near tie hides two completely different behaviours: one method that is sometimes perfect and eventually broken, against one that is never good and never varies.

Figures 5 and 6 are FashionMNIST, and the point of showing them is that they are visually almost interchangeable with figures 1 and 2. Same sawtooth under Advanced Lie, same collapse to the floor under Sparse Lie, same step up in the orange line. The paper draws the inference explicitly: the very similar curvature of the MNIST and FashionMNIST diagrams suggests that the detection is architecture rather than data driven. Two datasets with different content and different difficulty, run through the same fully connected model, produce the same defensive dynamics. That is a stronger statement than any single accuracy number, and it is only visible because the traces were plotted.

One caution before we leave these plots. Every one of them shows a single run of thirty rounds; there are no error bars, no repeated seeds, no shaded confidence bands. With only four possible values per point and one run per line, some of the fine structure, particularly the isolated spikes, could be luck. What is not luck is the sustained behaviour: a line that stays at 60 for thirteen consecutive rounds is not noise, and neither is a line that is flat for thirty.
