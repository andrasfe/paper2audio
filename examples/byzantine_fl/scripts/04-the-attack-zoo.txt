Chapter four. The attack zoo: thirteen ways to lie.

The paper evaluates against thirteen distinct Byzantine strategies, listed in its Table 1. I am going to walk through all of them, because the central result of the paper is not a single number, it is the observation that these thirteen attacks split into two families with opposite properties. If you understand the split, everything afterwards follows.

First, the notation, which is shared by almost every attack. Take the gradients that the honest clients would have sent this round. For each coordinate of the gradient vector, compute the mean across honest clients, and the standard deviation across honest clients. Call those two vectors mu and sigma. Mu is the honest consensus direction, coordinate by coordinate. Sigma measures how much the honest clients disagree, coordinate by coordinate, and it is the attacker's most valuable piece of intelligence, because it tells him exactly how much room he has to move in each coordinate without looking unusual. Most of these attacks are written as mu plus some multiple of sigma. Note the assumption baked in: the attacker is strong, he knows the honest statistics. That is the standard worst case in this literature.

Now the first family, which I will call the outlier producers. Gaussian noise: the malicious update is simply random noise drawn from a normal distribution with some scale alpha, ignoring the honest direction entirely. Scale: take the honest mean, multiply it by a factor alpha, and add a little noise; the direction is right but the magnitude is inflated. Sign flip: send the negative of the honest mean, which points the model backwards. Same value: every attacker sends exactly the same vector, the honest mean plus three sigma, so they are identical to each other and displaced from everyone else. Clustered: the attackers send the honest mean plus two beta sigma, where beta is the number of Byzantine clients, so they form a compact group sitting some distance away. Blatant lie: the honest mean plus a direction d, which is either plus one or minus one, times a factor z drawn uniformly between 1.75 and 3.25 sigma, plus extra noise whose scale is itself random, between one and four tenths of the average sigma. Targeted: pick a random 10 percent of the coordinates and push each of them 5 sigma above the mean, leaving the other 90 percent honest. Label flip: this one is different in kind, because the attacker does not craft a vector at all; he trains genuinely, but on corrupted labels, replacing each label y with y plus one modulo the number of classes. Whatever gradient that produces is what he sends. And stealthy: the honest mean plus very small noise, five hundredths of a unit of standard deviation, an update so close to the consensus that it is nearly harmless, which is the point, since it tests whether a defense produces false alarms.

Every attack in that first family shares a property: the malicious update lands measurably far from the honest cloud in ordinary distance, or lands in a suspiciously tight group of its own. That is exactly what MultiKrum's per-client scoring was designed to catch, and as we will see, it catches all of them, with 100 percent detection accuracy in most cases.

Now the second family, the evaders, and here the design philosophy inverts. The Lie attack, from the paper A Little Is Enough, sends the honest mean minus z times sigma, where z is a modest number chosen from the set 0.5, 1.0, 1.5 and 2.0. Read that carefully. The attacker moves in the direction opposite to honest consensus, but only by a fraction of the honest clients' own spread. He is inside the cloud. He is, by construction, no more unusual than an ordinary honest client with slightly atypical data. And because every attacker applies the same shift, they collectively drag the average, round after round, in a direction the model should not go.

The Advanced Lie attack, which the paper abbreviates as A L I E and I will call Advanced Lie, sharpens the idea. Instead of shifting every coordinate a little, it shifts a random 20 percent of the coordinates by a large amount, between three and eight sigma, with the sign negative 70 percent of the time, and leaves the other 80 percent of coordinates untouched at the honest mean. Now think about what Euclidean distance sees. Distance sums contributions over all coordinates, so a big displacement in a fifth of them, diluted across the other four fifths, produces a total distance that is unremarkable. But the direction of the resulting vector is genuinely different: it is tilted. That distinction, between how far a vector has moved and which way it points, is the hinge of this entire paper.

Sparse Lie takes the same idea to its extreme. It modifies only the coordinates in the top 5 percent by variance, the ones where honest clients disagree the most, and shifts each of them downward by a huge factor, between five and fifteen sigma. Ninety five percent of the vector is byte for byte the honest mean. The paper is explicit about why this is so hard: the Byzantine gradients are near identical to honest ones in 95 percent of dimensions. A distance based score can barely see it.

Finally, shuffle: take an honest gradient and randomly permute its coordinates. The set of numbers is unchanged, so the length of the vector is exactly right, and every summary statistic of the values is exactly right. What is destroyed is the correspondence between numbers and parameters, which is to say, the direction. This is the purest possible test of a direction sensitive defense, and it produces the most interesting architecture dependent behaviour in the results.

So there is the split. Nine attacks that produce outliers in magnitude, and four that preserve magnitude while corrupting direction or structure: Lie, Advanced Lie, Sparse Lie, and shuffle, with sign flip sitting awkwardly in between, since negating the mean preserves the length of the vector exactly while reversing its direction completely. The paper singles out those five as the evasion attacks, and Table 6 exists precisely to isolate them. Keep that list in mind. The remainder of the paper is, in effect, a search for a decision rule that sees direction as clearly as MultiKrum sees magnitude.
