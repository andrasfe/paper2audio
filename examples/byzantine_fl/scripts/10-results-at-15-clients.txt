Chapter ten. Results at fifteen clients: a tale of two families.

Tables 3, 4 and 5 report detection accuracy and F1 score for classical MultiKrum and for the plain QUBO method, on all thirteen attacks, averaged over thirty rounds, for MNIST, CIFAR-10 and FashionMNIST respectively. Rather than reciting three tables, let me read the MNIST one carefully and then tell you what changes on the other two.

MNIST, classical MultiKrum first. Perfect scores, 100 percent detection with 100 percent F1, on nine of the thirteen attacks: Gaussian noise, scale, targeted, clustered, same value, blatant lie, label flip, stealthy, and, as we will see in a moment, that list is exactly the outlier producing family. Against anything that moves an update conspicuously far from the crowd, or clones it into an identical block, per client Krum scoring is not merely good, it is flawless. Any narrative in which quantum optimization replaces the classical method has to get past that fact first.

Now the four attacks where it stumbles, on MNIST. Lie: 72.44 percent accuracy, F1 31.11. Advanced Lie: 81.33 percent, F1 53.33. Sparse Lie: 66.67 percent, F1 16.67. Shuffle: 69.33 percent, F1 23.33. And sign flip: 60 percent accuracy with F1 exactly zero, which, from the last chapter, you now recognize as the floor. Classical MultiKrum catches literally none of the sign flipping attackers on MNIST. That is worth a moment, because sign flip sounds like it should be trivially detectable. The reason it is not: negating the mean produces a vector of exactly the honest length, and with three attackers all negating, they land close to each other, forming their own dense neighbourhood. Each attacker's Krum score, computed from its nearest neighbours, is therefore low. The score cannot distinguish a tight cluster of colluders from tight honest agreement, exactly as chapter three warned.

Now the QUBO column, same dataset. On the five attacks classical struggles with, it wins every time. Advanced Lie goes from 81.33 to 95.11 percent, with F1 leaping from 53.33 to 87.78. Lie goes from 72.44 to 86.67 percent, F1 from 31.11 to 66.67. Sparse Lie from 66.67 to 80.89 percent, F1 from 16.67 to 52.22. Sign flip from 60 to 71.11 percent, and from zero F1 to 27.78, which the paper describes as a true positive rate of about 0.83, so it is catching most attackers now while making enough false accusations to keep the F1 modest. Only shuffle goes the other way on MNIST, dropping from 69.33 to 60 percent, that is, to the floor.

And now the flip side, which the paper does not hide. On the nine attacks where classical was perfect, the QUBO method is not merely worse, it is dramatically worse. Gaussian noise: 60 percent, F1 zero. Label flip: 60 percent, F1 zero. Scale: 74.67 percent, F1 36.67. Blatant lie: 83.11 percent. Stealthy: 82.67. Clustered and same value: 85.78 each. Targeted: 86.67. Against Gaussian noise, an attack you could detect by eye, the global optimizer catches nothing at all.

Why? Because the objective is about mutual relationships, not individual anomaly. When three attackers send pure random noise, they are far from the honest clients and far from each other, and the optimization is free to build a selected set that happens to include them, since nothing in the cost function says thou shalt not select a client that is individually absurd. Add the fact, which the authors state directly in their discussion, that with scattered Byzantine gradients the true minimum of the QUBO objective may itself include Byzantine updates, and you have the explanation. This is not the annealer failing to find the optimum. This is the optimum being the wrong thing to want.

So the honest summary of Table 3 is not that one method beats the other. It is that the two methods have almost disjoint competencies. The paper's own conclusion from this: taken together, these complementary strengths motivate an ensemble method as the most effective overall solution. That sentence is the hinge of the paper.

Now what changes on the other two datasets. FashionMNIST is nearly a carbon copy of MNIST, which is a genuinely informative result rather than a boring one. Advanced Lie is identical to two decimal places, 81.33 classical against 95.11 QUBO. Lie is 74.67 against 86.67, Sparse Lie 65.78 against 78.22, sign flip 60 against 69.78, shuffle 70.22 against 60. The paper's reading is that since MNIST and FashionMNIST share an architecture but differ in visual complexity, and the detection numbers barely move, detection performance depends primarily on model architecture and attack type rather than on how complex the images are.

CIFAR-10 is where the interesting deviations live, and there are three. First, Advanced Lie: the gap widens dramatically, 75.56 percent classical against 97.78 percent for QUBO, a 22.2 percentage point advantage, with F1 going from 38.89 to 94.44. Second, shuffle reverses: on MNIST the QUBO method lost, at 60 percent against 69.33, while on CIFAR-10 it wins convincingly, 90.67 percent against 70.22, with F1 from 25.56 up to 76.67. Third, the Lie attack reverses the other way: classical wins on CIFAR-10, 76 percent against 73.33.

The paper's explanation for all three turns on architecture, and it is worth following. A convolutional network encodes learned spatial filter patterns; its parameters are highly structured, and a coherent gradient has a distinctive directional signature. Permute those coordinates, as shuffle does, and you destroy filter level coherence, which shows up loudly in cosine distance even though the vector length is untouched. That is why shuffle is caught on CIFAR-10 and missed on the flat network. For the Lie attack the argument runs the other way: the fully connected models have far more parameters, one hundred fifty nine thousand against about seventeen thousand for the convolutional model, so a fixed perturbation budget spread over fewer parameters produces larger per coordinate shifts, which classical Euclidean scoring detects more easily. As the paper puts it, quantum formalism outperforms classical MultiKrum on the fully connected datasets but not on CIFAR-10, consistent with lower parameter dimensionality producing larger per coordinate shifts.

Table 6 is where all of this is compressed into a single comparison, and it is the table to remember. It isolates the five attacks on which classical detection falls below 100 percent, that is, the evasion family: Advanced Lie, Lie, Sparse Lie, sign flip, and shuffle, and reports classical against QUBO on all three datasets. The averages over those five attacks: on MNIST, 69.95 percent classical against 78.76 percent QUBO, a gain of 8.8 points. On CIFAR-10, 71.11 against 81.16, a gain of 10.0 points. On FashionMNIST, 70.40 against 77.96, a gain of 7.6 points. Consistent across architectures, largest on the convolutional one.

That is the whole fifteen client story, and I want to state its shape plainly before we move on, because it would be easy to hear a list of percentages and miss the argument. The claim being established is not that a quantum annealer is a better Byzantine filter. It is that magnitude and direction are two different pieces of evidence; that classical MultiKrum reads magnitude and is blind to direction; that the QUBO formulation with cosine distance reads direction and is careless about magnitude; and that the attacks split cleanly along the same line. Everything after this point is engineering: how to use both pieces of evidence at once, at a scale where you cannot afford to be wasteful.
