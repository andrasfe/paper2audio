TITLE: Quantum in Finance: Three Case Studies on the Feasibility of
Quantum Computing for Financial Workloads

[abstract]

We evaluate the feasibility of quantum computing for finance through
three pre-registered case studies on a century of public market data
(Kenneth French daily portfolios, 1926--2026), pairing each of three
quantum algorithms with the financial use case it most plausibly serves:
(i) the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) for compact
portfolio selection via dependence-structure QUBOs; (ii) quantum
amplitude estimation (QAE) for Monte-Carlo tail-risk measurement; and
(iii) quantum-enhanced Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) for sampling on
rugged optimization landscapes. Every study uses frozen decision rules,
certified or measured classical baselines, and adversarial controls.
The verdicts: (i) QAOA offers no advantage on financial QUBOs---market
crises simplify the problems rather than hardening them, the
ensembles are self-averaging, and QAOA's cost scales $1.3$--$8\times$
worse per variable than simulated annealing exactly where residual
hardness lives; (ii) QAE's quadratic speedup is real and directly
observed in simulation (queries $\propto\eps^{-1.03}$ vs Monte Carlo's
$\eps^{-2.00}$), but standard risk estimates cost under a second
classically, placing the wall-clock crossover at economically
meaningless precision; a derived workload-selection criterion shows
what a viable QAE workload must look like; (iii) quantum-enhanced MCMC
passes its positive control decisively---a $\sim$$7\times$
relaxation-exponent advantage on cold random spin glasses, the
strongest quantum-favorable measurement in the program---but financial
instances at exactly-verifiable sizes are not rugged enough to benefit.
We conclude that the typical financial optimization use case is a poor
QAOA target for a structural reason (self-averaging, homogenizing
dependence ensembles), while emphasizing that this does not preclude
practical quantum value: the observed QAE query scaling and the
validated quantum-MCMC mixing advantage identify the specific workload
properties---expensive-per-scenario estimation and cold rugged
landscapes at scale---that a future practical application must exhibit.

[end abstract]

== Introduction ==

Quantum computing is regularly proposed as a future accelerator for
financial workloads, with portfolio optimization and Monte-Carlo risk
measurement cited most often. These proposals typically rest on
asymptotic arguments; whether any advantage survives contact with real
market data, realistic precision targets, and competent classical
baselines is an empirical question. Because quantum hardware is not
ready for commercial use, that question cannot yet be settled by
deployment---but it can be settled, workload by workload, through
controlled feasibility studies that measure the quantities on which any
future advantage must depend: scaling exponents, certified optimality
gaps, per-operation cost ratios, and precision requirements.

This paper reports three such case studies, executed under a common
discipline: designs and decision rules frozen in pre-registrations
before results existed; classical baselines that are certified (exact
optimization at zero MIP gap) or measured (replicated Monte-Carlo cost)
rather than assumed; adversarial controls that attribute every effect
to a mechanism; and honesty clauses that separate what was measured
(query counts, chain steps, exponents) from what was not (wall-clock
hardware performance). No quantum hardware time was consumed; all
quantum objects were simulated exactly or resource-counted from
verified circuit constructions.

Contributions: (1) a regime-conditional hardness study of data-driven
financial QUBOs across the 2008 and 2020 crises, refuting---with the
sign reversed---the hypothesis that crises produce quantum-relevant
hardness; (2) a verification-gated resource estimate for QAE tail-risk
measurement on backtested models, with the quadratic speedup observed
directly and a quantitative workload-selection criterion derived; (3)
the first application of the quantum-enhanced MCMC proposal to
finance-derived instances, with its advantage reproduced on controls
and its inapplicability at verifiable financial sizes explained; and
(4) a cross-cutting account of why typical financial ensembles
resist quantum optimization---self-averaging---and what would have to
be different for practical value to emerge.

== Three Quantum Algorithms ==

-- QAOA --

The Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm encodes a quadratic
unconstrained binary optimization problem (QUBO) into a cost
Hamiltonian and alternates $p$ layers of cost-phase and mixer
evolution, controlled by $2p$ angles $(\gamma,\beta)$
 [ref: farhi2014]. Performance depends entirely on the angles; the
practical hope is the fixed-angle protocol  [ref: shaydulin2024]:
on structured problem families, optimal angles concentrate across
instances and sizes, so they can be trained cheaply on small instances
and deployed on large ones without per-instance optimization. Whether
angles concentrate is measurable classically---the circular standard
deviation $\sigma$ of trained angles across sub-instances---making
transferability a screenable property  [ref: qbrainfs2026].

-- Quantum amplitude estimation --

QAE estimates an expectation encoded in a quantum amplitude with error
$\eps$ using $O(1/\eps)$ oracle calls, versus $O(1/\eps^2)$ samples for
classical Monte Carlo---an unconditional, provably optimal quadratic
speedup  [ref: woerner2019,grinko2021]. Iterative variants (IQAE) avoid
phase-estimation overhead. The oracle for a risk workload comprises
state loaders for the market distribution, fixed-point payoff
arithmetic, and a comparator; every component has countable
fault-tolerant cost, so end-to-end feasibility is a resource-estimation
exercise  [ref: chakrabarti2021,babbush2021].

-- Quantum-enhanced MCMC --

Rather than preparing solutions directly, this family uses a quantum
circuit as the proposal distribution inside a classical
Metropolis--Hastings loop  [ref: layden2023]: from state $|x\rangle$,
evolve under a mixed problem/mixer Hamiltonian, measure to obtain the
proposal $y$, and accept classically. The proposal is symmetric, so
detailed balance---and therefore correctness---holds regardless of
noise; hardware imperfection degrades only the speed. Measured hardware
evidence exists for a polynomial mixing-time advantage on rugged
(spin-glass) energy landscapes at low temperature, precisely the regime
where classical local-move chains freeze.

== Three Financial Use Cases ==

Quantum hardware is not commercially usable today; the value of a
use-case study is therefore not deployment but selection:
identifying, with measured constants, which workloads could ever repay
quantum investment and which never will. The three cases below were
chosen because each is the canonical pairing for its algorithm, each is
economically real, and each is testable end-to-end on public data.

-- Compact portfolio selection (QAOA) --

Select $k$ of $n$ assets that are jointly relevant to the market factor
and minimally redundant---index tracking, hedge-basket construction,
and feature selection all share this shape. The natural encoding is a
dependence-structure QUBO built from distance-correlation (DC) or
mutual-information (MI) matrices  [ref: samanta2025]. It is a good
quantum testbed because the instance ensemble is generated by the
market itself and varies with regime: a widely-voiced hypothesis holds
that crisis-period correlation structure makes these QUBOs rugged and
classically hard, which would be exactly the opening a quantum
optimizer needs. Whether that is true had not been measured.

-- Tail-risk measurement (QAE) --

Value-at-Risk and expected shortfall ($\cvar$) are computed by
Monte-Carlo simulation across the industry, at enormous aggregate
compute cost. QAE's quadratic speedup applies directly. It is a good
testbed because everything is quantifiable: the precision a risk desk
actually needs (basis points), the classical cost (measurable to the
nanosecond), and the quantum cost (countable to the $T$-gate)---so the
feasibility question reduces to arithmetic once the constants are
pinned down on a calibrated, backtested model.

-- Sampling on rugged landscapes (quantum MCMC) --

Wherever finance needs low-temperature samples or near-optimal
configurations on rugged objective surfaces---combinatorial allocation
under complex constraints, Bayesian posteriors with multimodal
structure---classical local-move MCMC freezes. Quantum-enhanced MCMC is
a good testbed because it is the one candidate whose advantage has
hardware evidence on the right landscape class, because correctness is
noise-robust, and because case study 1 supplies naturally-occurring
financial instances whose hardness is independently certified.

== Common Methodology ==

Data: Kenneth French daily portfolios---49 industries and 100
size/book-to-market portfolios (10$\times$10), 1926--2026, committed
with the code. Windows of 126 trading days define instrument ensembles:
calm (2006, 2019) versus crisis (GFC autumn 2008, COVID spring 2020),
plus rolling windows for trajectories. All QUBOs use one construction
(relevance/redundancy form, pool by relevance, soft cardinality with
calibrated penalty, unit normalization). Classical baselines: Gurobi
with exact eigenvalue-shift convexification at zero MIP gap
(certification), vectorized simulated annealing (SA) with
time-to-solution $\tts$ (sweeps $\times$ restarts to 99% success),
and replicated vectorized Monte Carlo. Every study's windows, metrics,
controls, and decision rules were frozen in committed pre-registrations
before results existed; the few amendments (all pre-result, all
cost- or artifact-driven) are documented in place, including one
positive-control gate failure that invalidated and corrected a study
design (Sec. [sec:cs3]).

== Case Study 1: Portfolio-Selection QUBOs and QAOA ==

-- Does crisis create hardness? No---the reverse --

Across 114 pre-registered cells on the 49-industry universe, every
hardness indicator moves opposite to the crisis-ruggedization
hypothesis (Table [tab:regime], Fig. [fig:traj]): SA reaches
the certified optimum $12$--$1000\times$ faster on crisis windows,
trained QAOA angles concentrate $2$--$3\times$ tighter, and descent
landscapes carry up to $20\times$ fewer local minima. Controls
attribute the mechanism: permuting coupling values (preserving their
histogram) preserves hardness, while destroying cross-dependence
removes it---so hardness lives in the \emph{heterogeneity of the
coupling distribution}, which a crisis destroys as all correlations
converge on the market factor (off-diagonal coefficient of variation
$0.17\!\to\!0.06$). Self-averaging probes show optimum-energy
fluctuations shrinking with size everywhere: the ensembles are
statistically homogeneous, with no spin-glass signature.

-- The concentration--hardness trade-off --

One variable---coupling heterogeneity---controls classical hardness and
fixed-angle transferability with opposite signs. Calm-period DC
ensembles are the only classically hard objects in the family, and
precisely there trained angles sit at the non-concentrating plateau
($\sigma\approx1.0$--$1.5$), excluding the fixed-angle protocol;
where angles concentrate (crises), SA solves the instances in
milliseconds. On matched sub-instances, direct per-instance QAOA
shots-to-solution scales as $2^{0.87--1.25n}$ versus SA's
$2^{0.14--0.16n}$: a $5$--$8\times$ exponent deficit
(Fig. [fig:scaling]).

\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_scaling.png}

-- A richer universe narrows---but does not close---the gap --

On the 100-portfolio (size$\times$value) universe the instances get
qualitatively harder: convexified certification fails at 600\,s in
13/16 cells, and SA fails to find calm-window pool-100 optima
(best-of-1024 lands $1.1$--$1.7%$ high)---while crisis windows stay
easy, confirming the regime effect at scale. A full-budget
admissibility study found angle concentration genuinely improved
($\sigma_{p2}=0.57$--$0.89$) and the QAOA/SA exponent ratio collapsed
from $5$--$8\times$ to $\sim$$1.3\times$ ($2^{1.01--1.03n}$ vs
$2^{0.74--0.82n}$)---the first trend in the program pointing
toward rather than away from an eventual crossover family. But the
decisive transfer gate failed by $52$--$122\times$: concentration
across same-size instances did not extend across sizes, so no cheap
deployment protocol exists, and per-instance training is not a
protocol. \textbf{Verdict: no QAOA case on financial dependence QUBOs;
the residual hardness is real but classical methods keep a decisive
scaling edge wherever it lives.}

== Case Study 2: QAE for Tail Risk ==

-- Setup and verification --

Workload: 99% one-day $\cvar$ of the equal-weight 49-industry
portfolio to $\eps\in\{10,2,0.5\}\bp$, on a one-factor Student-$t$
model ($\nu\approx4.7$) whose 99% VaR passes a Kupiec coverage
backtest out-of-sample (2021--2025, $p=0.35$). Registers were sized by
a discretization-honesty gate (grid bias $<\eps/2$ against a
$4\times$ finer grid). The oracle (loaders, weighted adder, comparator)
was simulated end-to-end at an 18-qubit truncation through IQAE against
exact enumeration: every estimate inside its confidence interval, and
the measured query scaling was $\eps^{-1.03}$ (quantum) versus
$\eps^{-2.00}$ (classical MC)---\emph{the quadratic speedup observed
directly on the data-calibrated oracle}, with quantum already using
$6\times$ fewer oracle calls than MC samples at $\eps=10^{-3}$.
Resources were counted two independent ways (analytic vs constructed
circuit; the $25%$ discrepancy is documented and the smaller count
used, favoring quantum).

The machine itself is surprisingly plausible---282 logical qubits,
under $10^{6}$ physical at the optimistic corner---but the classical
baseline is $0.4$ CPU-seconds (Table [tab:qae],
Fig. [fig:frontier]): classical wins by $\sim$$5$ orders of
magnitude at every meaningful precision. The general criterion is

[equation]

\eps < \eps^{*} = \frac{\sigma\,c_{s}}{\kappa\,c_{q}},

[end equation]

where $c_s$/$c_q$ are per-sample/per-oracle-call costs; measured
$c_q/c_s\approx10^{8}$--$10^{9}$ here. Two nested-simulation cells
(inner Monte-Carlo repricing of option books) tested the criterion's
favorable side: neither cleared it---one because pooled European
baskets are statistically easy inside ($N_{\rm in}\approx200$--$7000$),
and the pair together showed the verdict is dominated by the inner
pricing-tolerance convention on premium-heterogeneous books.
\textbf{Verdict: the speedup is real; standard risk workloads are
$\sim$$10^{5}\times$ too cheap per scenario for it to matter, and any
viable QAE application must first exhibit expensive, statistically hard
inner valuation under a pre-committed tolerance schedule.}

== Case Study 3: Quantum-Enhanced MCMC ==

-- Design, and a gate that worked --

For instance sizes $n\le12$ the full $2^{n}\times2^{n}$ transition
matrix of each chain is computed exactly: quantum proposal
(averaged $|\langle y|e^{-iHt}|x\rangle|^{2}$ over randomized
$(\gamma,t)$), single-flip, uniform, and random-$k$-flip proposals,
identical Metropolis acceptance, three temperatures, absolute spectral
gaps by dense diagonalization; detailed balance verified to
$10^{-15}$. The first version of this study \emph{failed its own
positive-control gate}: an instance-adaptive temperature grew with
$\sqrt{n}$, corrupting slope comparisons. The pre-registered response
---documented failure, one amendment (intensive temperature
$T_k = 2^{-k}J_{\rm rms}$), full re-run---is reported as part of the
result, since it is the controls discipline working as intended.

On the control ensemble the Nature-paper effect reproduces decisively
(Fig. [fig:qmcmc], Table [tab:qmcmc]): as temperature falls the
single-flip exponent explodes ($0.67\!\to\!1.21\!\to\!2.24$) while the
quantum exponent stays flat ($\approx0.3$)---a $\sim$$7\times$
advantage and the strongest quantum-favorable measurement in this
program. On the finance ensemble the frozen verdict is \emph{no
evidence}: the instances at $n\le12$ are simply not glassy (single-flip
mixes with exponents $0.03$--$0.22$), so there is no bottleneck to
relieve. The explanation ties the program together: FF100 calm-window
hardness is a property of the full coupled pool ($n\ge32$) and
is not inherited by small sub-instances---while exact spectral gaps
cannot go beyond $n\approx12$--$14$. The validated method and the
validated hard instances do not yet overlap in scale; sampled
mixing-time estimation at $n=16$--$32$ is the identified bridging
experiment. Per the pre-registered honesty clause, exponents count
chain steps: a quantum step is a circuit (microseconds at best) versus
$O(n)$ classical flops (nanoseconds), so even the control result is
evidence about future advantage on cold rugged landscapes, not a
present-day wall-clock claim.

== Discussion ==

Three cross-cutting quantities decided every verdict, and none of them
is visible from asymptotics alone. (1) Where hardness lives:
financial dependence ensembles are self-averaging and homogenize under
stress; their hardness, when present, is a collective property of large
coupled pools. (2) Per-operation cost ratios: a quadratic query
advantage is erased by a $10^{8}$ per-call handicap until precision
demands become unphysical---Eq. [eq:criterion] converts this into
a workload screen. (3) Scale overlap: a quantum method validated
on controls is only evidence for a use case if the sizes where the
method is measurable intersect the sizes where the use case is hard;
in case study 3 they do not, yet. These three screens---structural
hardness, cost-ratio, scale overlap---are, we suggest, the minimum a
credible quantum-finance proposal should pass before hardware
experiments are contemplated, and each is measurable classically at
modest cost.

== Conclusions ==

For the typical financial optimization use case, our evidence indicates
QAOA is the wrong tool for a structural reason, not an engineering one:
data-driven financial QUBOs are self-averaging---instance-to-
instance fluctuations shrink with size, typical instances are alike and
benign, and the heterogeneous, glassy structure that quantum
optimizers would need does not survive aggregation, least of all in
crises, which homogenize the dependence structure and make the
problems easier. Where residual hardness does appear (calm
periods, wide universes), classical annealing retains a decisive
scaling edge and the fixed-angle protocol fails its transfer gates.

This does not mean quantum computing will not be practical---in finance
or elsewhere. Two of our three case studies produced genuinely
quantum-favorable measurements: the QAE quadratic speedup observed
end-to-end on a data-calibrated oracle, and a $7\times$ mixing-exponent
advantage for quantum-enhanced MCMC on cold rugged landscapes,
reproduced under gates from independent hardware evidence. What our
program shows is that practicality is a workload-selection
problem with measurable acceptance criteria: per-scenario classical
cost exceeding $\sim$$10^{5}\times$ vectorized arithmetic for
estimation workloads; genuinely non-self-averaging, pool-scale rugged
structure for optimization and sampling workloads; and overlap between
the sizes where quantum methods can be validated and the sizes where
the target problem is hard. Financial workloads that clear these
screens were not found among the standard candidates tested here---but
the screens themselves, and the validated measurement machinery behind
them, are the constructive product: they tell practitioners exactly
what to measure before spending a dollar of quantum hardware time, and
they will identify the first genuine application if and when one
exists.

pre-registrations, checkpointed results ($\sim$700 cells/instances
across five studies), and amendment history are in the accompanying
