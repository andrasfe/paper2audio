Quantum computing is regularly proposed as a future accelerator for
financial workloads, with portfolio optimization and Monte-Carlo risk
measurement cited most often. These proposals typically rest on
asymptotic arguments; whether any advantage survives contact with real
market data, realistic precision targets, and competent classical
baselines is an empirical question. Because quantum hardware is not
ready for commercial use, that question cannot yet be settled by
deployment---but it can be settled, workload by workload, through
controlled feasibility studies that measure the quantities on which any
future advantage must depend: scaling exponents, certified optimality
gaps, per-operation cost ratios, and precision requirements.

This paper reports three such case studies, executed under a common
discipline: designs and decision rules frozen in pre-registrations
before results existed; classical baselines that are certified (exact
optimization at zero MIP gap) or measured (replicated Monte-Carlo cost)
rather than assumed; adversarial controls that attribute every effect
to a mechanism; and honesty clauses that separate what was measured
(query counts, chain steps, exponents) from what was not (wall-clock
hardware performance). No quantum hardware time was consumed; all
quantum objects were simulated exactly or resource-counted from
verified circuit constructions.

Contributions: (1) a regime-conditional hardness study of data-driven
financial QUBOs across the 2008 and 2020 crises, refuting---with the
sign reversed---the hypothesis that crises produce quantum-relevant
hardness; (2) a verification-gated resource estimate for QAE tail-risk
measurement on backtested models, with the quadratic speedup observed
directly and a quantitative workload-selection criterion derived; (3)
the first application of the quantum-enhanced MCMC proposal to
finance-derived instances, with its advantage reproduced on controls
and its inapplicability at verifiable financial sizes explained; and
(4) a cross-cutting account of why typical financial ensembles
resist quantum optimization---self-averaging---and what would have to
be different for practical value to emerge.
