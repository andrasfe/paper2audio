Let us start with the motivation. For about a decade, quantum computing has been marketed as a future accelerator for finance. The two applications you hear about most are portfolio optimization, meaning choosing the best combination of assets, and Monte Carlo risk simulation, meaning estimating how much money a portfolio could lose. The sales pitch usually rests on what mathematicians call asymptotic arguments. An asymptotic argument says: as the problem gets infinitely large, the quantum method scales better than the classical method. The trouble is that real problems are not infinitely large, real quantum hardware is slow and error prone, and real classical computers are astonishingly fast and cheap. So the honest question is not, does a quantum speedup exist on paper. The honest question is, for the actual problems a bank runs, with the actual precision a bank needs, against the best classical code a competent engineer would write, could a quantum computer ever win. That is an empirical question, and this paper treats it as one.

There is a catch, of course. Quantum hardware today is not ready for commercial use. You cannot settle the question by deployment. But, and this is the central methodological idea of the paper, you can settle it workload by workload through controlled feasibility studies, because any future advantage must depend on quantities you can measure right now, classically. Those quantities are things like: how fast does the difficulty of the problem grow as it gets bigger, which is called a scaling exponent. How good is the classical competition, which you can certify exactly. How expensive is one quantum operation compared to one classical operation. And how precise does the answer actually need to be. If you pin those numbers down, the feasibility verdict becomes arithmetic.
