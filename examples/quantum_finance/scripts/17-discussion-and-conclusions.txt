Let us assemble the whole picture, as the discussion section does. Three cross-cutting quantities decided every verdict, and none of them is visible from asymptotic theory alone. First, where hardness lives. Financial dependence ensembles are self-averaging and homogenize under stress; their hardness, when present at all, is a collective property of large coupled pools. Second, per-operation cost ratios. A quadratic query advantage is erased by a ten to the eighth per-call handicap until the precision demanded becomes unphysical; the criterion equation converts that observation into a screen anyone can apply. Third, scale overlap. A quantum method validated on control problems is evidence for a use case only if the sizes where the method can be measured intersect the sizes where the use case is actually hard; in case study three they do not yet. The paper proposes these three, structural hardness, cost ratio, scale overlap, as the minimum screening battery any quantum-finance proposal should pass before hardware experiments are even contemplated. Each is measurable classically, at modest cost. That checklist is arguably the paper's most practically valuable contribution.

The conclusions, then, in the paper's own logic. For the typical financial optimization use case, Q A O A is the wrong tool for a structural reason, not an engineering one. Data-driven financial QUBOs are self-averaging: fluctuations shrink with size, typical instances are alike and benign, and the heterogeneous, glassy structure a quantum optimizer would need does not survive aggregation, least of all in crises, which homogenize dependence and make the problems easier, the exact opposite of the folk hypothesis. Where residual hardness does appear, in calm periods and wide universes, classical annealing keeps a decisive scaling edge and the fixed-angle deployment protocol fails its transfer gates. If you remember one sentence from this entire talk, make it this one: the story quantum optimization needs, crisis makes financial problems quantum-hard, is measurably backwards.

But, and the paper is emphatic here, this does not mean quantum computing will not be practical, in finance or elsewhere. Two of the three case studies produced genuinely quantum-favorable measurements: the quadratic speedup of amplitude estimation observed end to end on a data-calibrated, backtest-verified oracle, and a seven-fold mixing-exponent advantage for quantum-enhanced M C M C on cold rugged landscapes, reproduced under pre-registered gates.

And here, let me put the brightest highlighter in the whole course on one of those two, because if you carry a single forward looking message off this recording, it should be this one. The most promising quantum experiment in this entire program is quantum enhanced Markov chain Monte Carlo. Weigh what it uniquely has going for it. Its advantage is measured, not projected: a seven fold relaxation exponent advantage, in exact arithmetic, under a pre registered gate, on precisely the landscape class where classical methods are provably miserable. Its correctness is noise robust by construction, the one property that makes an algorithm deployable on imperfect hardware, because errors can only slow it, never corrupt it. Its target regime, cold sampling on rugged landscapes, is a genuine classical weakness, not a strength, so it does not have to outrun vectorized arithmetic the way amplitude estimation does. And it sits exactly one specified, pre registerable, classically executable experiment, sampled mixing times at sixteen to thirty two spins on the certified hard calm window instances, away from its first honest test on financial problems. No other quantum approach in finance today has all four of those properties at once. When you hear the research agenda chapter later, that bridging experiment is priority one for exactly this reason; and if you ever allocate a research budget in this space, that is the experiment to fund first. What the program shows is that practicality is a workload-selection problem with measurable acceptance criteria. For estimation workloads: per-scenario classical cost must exceed roughly ten to the fifth times vectorized arithmetic. For optimization and sampling workloads: genuinely non-self-averaging, pool-scale rugged structure. And always: overlap between the sizes where quantum methods can be validated and the sizes where the target problem is hard. Standard financial candidates cleared none of these screens. But the screens themselves, and the validated measurement machinery behind them, tell practitioners exactly what to measure before spending a dollar of quantum hardware time, and they will identify the first genuine application if and when one exists.
