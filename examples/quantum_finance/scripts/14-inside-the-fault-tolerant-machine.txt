You have just heard that a plausible machine, two hundred eighty two logical qubits, some eight hundred thousand physical ones, still loses to a laptop by five orders of magnitude. Before case study three, let us take the guided tour inside that hypothetical machine, because every number in the frontier table was produced by a small set of formulas you can genuinely understand, and because knowing where the costs concentrate tells you exactly what hardware progress would and would not change the verdict.

Start on the factory floor: the surface code, the error correcting architecture nearly every roadmap assumes. Picture a large checkerboard of physical qubits. Half of them, the data qubits, collectively hold one logical qubit's worth of quantum information, smeared out non locally so no single physical failure can corrupt it. The other half are measurement qubits, and in every cycle of the machine's clock, roughly a microsecond, each one performs a small ritual with its four neighbors that extracts one parity bit: did an error just flip something around here, yes or no? This stream of parity bits, the syndrome, flows to a classical decoder, which plays a real time game of connect the dots, inferring the most likely pattern of physical errors and tracking the corrections. The genius of the scheme is that the logical information is topological: to corrupt it, a chain of physical errors must stretch all the way across the patch, and the probability of that drops exponentially with the patch width. That width is the code distance, d, and a patch of distance d needs about two d squared physical qubits.

Here is the actual suppression formula the paper uses, and it is worth hearing once. The logical error rate per logical qubit per cycle equals one tenth, times, the physical error rate divided by one percent, raised to the power of, d plus one, over two. Read it as a recipe. The one percent inside the parentheses is the threshold: if your physical error rate is exactly at threshold, the parenthesis equals one, and increasing d does nothing; error correction merely treads water. At a physical error rate of one in a thousand, the parenthesis is one tenth, so every increase of the distance by two buys you another factor of ten in reliability. At one in ten thousand, the optimistic corner, the parenthesis is one hundredth, and every step of two in distance buys a factor of one hundred. That is the leverage that makes megaquop and teraquop machines conceivable at all. Sizing the machine is then a budget exercise: the paper demands that the expected number of logical faults across the entire computation, the T count, times the distance, times the per cycle logical error rate, times the number of logical qubits, stays below one percent, and picks the smallest odd distance that satisfies it. For the headline job, that lands the distance in the mid teens, hence a couple of thousand physical qubits per logical qubit, hence eight hundred thousand physical qubits for two hundred eighty two logical ones plus factories.

Why do T gates, and only T gates, appear in that budget? Recall from the machine room chapter: the surface code executes Clifford operations essentially for free, by software bookkeeping and patch surgery, but the T gate, the one eighth turn that unlocks universality, must be injected using a consumable resource, a magic state, and raw magic states inherit the physical error rate, which is far too dirty. The fix is distillation, and the standard unit is the fifteen to one protocol: take fifteen noisy magic states, run them through a small Clifford circuit that is itself protected by the code, and out comes a single magic state whose error is proportional to the cube of the input error, thirty five times p cubed, to be precise. Cubing a small number is a wonderful thing: one in a thousand becomes about three in one hundred million in a single round. If that is not clean enough, feed fifteen once distilled states into a second round, fifteen squared, two hundred twenty five raw states per output, and cube again. Each factory is a dedicated block of the checkerboard, about fifteen patches of area, whose only job is to churn out these states on a conveyor belt.

Now the machine's speed limit, and the formula behind every wall clock number you heard. In the standard model of fault tolerant computation, the T starved pipeline, the computation advances exactly as fast as magic states arrive. Wall clock time equals, total T count, times the code distance, because consuming one magic state takes about d clock cycles, times the cycle time, divided by the number of factories running in parallel. Plug in the headline job at the optimistic corner: ten to the eleven T gates, distance around fifteen, one hundred nanosecond cycles, one hundred factories, and out comes roughly fifteen hundred seconds, the zero point four three hours in the table. This is where the frontier figure's band comes from: sweep the physical error rate between one in a thousand and one in ten thousand, the cycle time between a microsecond and a hundred nanoseconds, and the factory count between one and one hundred, and the quantum cost line fans out into a band spanning about three orders of magnitude. The classical line, meanwhile, is a single measured point per precision, no assumptions required. When a band whose most favorable edge assumes everything going right still floats five decades above the measured classical line, corner arguments are over.

Where exactly did those ten to the eleven T gates come from? The paper's decomposition is unusually clean, and it relocates the villain. Only twenty six percent of the T budget is the arithmetic, the adders and comparators, the part that feels like computing. Seventy four percent is state preparation: loading the market probability distributions into amplitudes. Each loader uses the textbook uniformly controlled rotation construction, two to the m minus two arbitrary angle rotations for an m qubit register, and every arbitrary rotation must be compiled into Clifford plus T at about three T gates per digit of accuracy, roughly forty T gates per rotation at the tolerances required. Multiply by two loaders per Grover iterate, times four hundred twenty thousand oracle calls, and the input interface swallows three quarters of the machine's entire lifetime output of magic states. This is the H H L data loading caveat from the algorithm zoo, no longer a caveat but a line item with a price. It also identifies the real research lever: better loading constructions, Q R O M lookup tables, block encodings from quantum signal processing, or hybrid schemes that exploit the distribution's smoothness, could plausibly cut the total three or four fold. Worth doing, the paper says, and then does the arithmetic: three or four fold against a gap of one hundred thousand fold. The verdict is loader proof.

One last perspective before we leave the machine room. Everything in this chapter, the distance formula, the factory throughput, the pipeline model, is textbook standard, deliberately so: the paper wants its resource estimates comparable to everyone else's, and errs in quantum's favor at each fork, smaller of two gate counts, most optimistic corner, free classical decoding. The point of the tour is not that these machines are impossible; at under a million physical qubits, the headline machine is squarely on published roadmaps for the mid twenty thirties. The point is sharper: even granting the machine, the workload has to deserve it, and deserving it is a property you can compute today, with the criterion equation, before the machine exists. Keep that in mind as we head for the one algorithm family in this program that passed its own quantum test: quantum enhanced M C M C. Its toolbox gets the full resolution treatment first.
