Now the algorithm zoo: a tour of the quantum algorithms that matter, what each one genuinely promises, and the chain of papers that led finance to the front of the queue. This context will let you place the three case studies on the map, and judge for yourself how representative they are.

The story starts in nineteen ninety four with Shor's algorithm, which factors large numbers exponentially faster than any known classical method, and thereby breaks the R S A cryptography protecting most of the internet. Shor's algorithm is why governments fund this field. But note two things about it. First, its speedup is exponential, not quadratic: doubling the problem size adds a constant factor to the quantum cost but squares the classical cost, so no per operation handicap can save the classical side at scale. Second, it needs an error corrected machine, millions of physical qubits for cryptographically relevant numbers; nobody runs Shor on today's hardware except on toy numbers. Keep that pairing in mind, exponential speedup plus fault tolerance, because it is the gold standard everything else gets compared to.

Nineteen ninety six brought Grover's algorithm: search an unstructured list of N items in only square root of N steps. It is provably optimal, beautifully general, and only quadratic, and that phrase, only quadratic, will haunt this whole talk. Amplitude estimation, the engine of our case study two, is Grover's sibling: the same rotation trick, used not to find a marked item but to count, to estimate the probability of a marked outcome, quadratically faster than sampling. Meanwhile Grover style search applied to optimization gives quadratic speedups over brute force, but brute force is rarely the classical state of the art, which is exactly the gap simulated annealing exploited in case study one.

Two thousand nine added the H H L algorithm, solving linear systems of equations in time logarithmic in the matrix size, an exponential speedup on paper, and the seed of a decade of quantum machine learning proposals. But H H L carries famous fine print, spelled out in a widely cited caution by Aaronson: the input vector must be loaded into amplitudes, which can cost as much as the classical solve; the matrix must be well conditioned; and the output is a quantum state, not a printed vector, so reading out all the answers costs the speedup back. Every one of those caveats will reappear, in measured form, in this paper's loader counts. The lesson generalizes: asymptotic speedups live or die on their input output interfaces.

Then came the NISQ era workarounds. Around twenty fourteen, variational algorithms appeared: the variational quantum eigensolver for chemistry, and Farhi, Goldstone, and Gutmann's Q A O A for optimization, both designed as hybrids where a classical optimizer tunes a short quantum circuit's parameters. They fit noisy hardware because the circuits are shallow, but they inherit a new disease: the tuning loop itself. Training cost, and a phenomenon called barren plateaus where gradients vanish exponentially, mean the classical wrapper can eat the quantum filling. The fixed angle discovery, angles concentrating across instances on structured families, was the field's best answer to that disease, and the strongest published evidence for it is worth knowing precisely: in twenty twenty four, Shaydulin and coauthors showed that on a specific structured problem called low autocorrelation binary sequences, L A B S, fixed angle Q A O A achieves a measured scaling exponent better than the best known classical solver's, about two to the zero point two two n versus two to the zero point three four n. That is a real, measured quantum scaling advantage, on one problem family, with transferred angles. Case study one is, in essence, the question: does anything like L A B S happen on problem families that markets actually generate? You already know the study's answer, and you now know exactly what published result it was calibrated against.

On the estimation side, the lineage into finance runs through four papers. Brassard and coauthors formalized amplitude estimation in two thousand two. In twenty nineteen, Woerner and Egger's quantum risk analysis showed how to load a loss distribution, compute value at risk and expected shortfall in superposition, and extract them by amplitude estimation, the exact template our case study two implements and stress tests. Grinko and coauthors' iterative amplitude estimation, twenty twenty one, removed the expensive phase estimation scaffolding; that is the I Q A E variant the paper runs. And in twenty twenty one, Chakrabarti and coauthors published a threshold for quantum advantage in derivative pricing, a careful resource count concluding that beating classical Monte Carlo on realistic autocallable books would need roughly seven and a half thousand logical qubits running at megahertz logical clock rates, far beyond near term machines but not absurd. What this paper adds to that lineage is the other blade of the scissors: not just how big must the quantum machine be, but how cheap is the classical competitor, measured to the nanosecond on the identical model, plus a backtested calibration and an end to end verified circuit. It is the difference between costing one side of a trade and marking both sides to market.

A separate warning shot came from Babbush and coauthors in twenty twenty one, titled focus beyond quadratic speedups for error corrected quantum advantage. Their argument, from general resource modeling: once you pay error correction overheads, a quadratic speedup with realistic constants only wins at problem sizes requiring months or years of runtime, so practical advantage most likely requires quartic or better, or exponential, speedups. At the time it was a projection. This paper's case study two is, to my knowledge, one of the cleanest empirical instantiations of that thesis on a real workload: quadratic speedup measured, verified, and still five orders of magnitude short, with the crossover at ten to the minus nine precision. Theory predicted the shape; this program measured the constants.

Third branch: sampling. Quantum enhanced M C M C, the Layden and coauthors Nature paper of twenty twenty three, is a different philosophy from everything above. It does not ask the quantum computer to be right, only to be inspired: propose moves, and let a classical accept reject rule guarantee correctness. That noise robustness made it the first algorithm family with measured hardware evidence of a scaling improvement on a task, low temperature sampling of rugged landscapes, that classical computing is genuinely bad at. It is also young: the evidence is at small sizes, the wall clock question is open, and its financial applicability had never been tested before this program did so in case study three.

And a word on the elephant from an adjacent zoo: quantum annealers, the D Wave machines, thousands of analog qubits that physically cool toward a QUBO's minimum rather than running gate circuits. They have been sold into finance pilots for a decade. Everything case study one measures about problem structure applies to them with full force: an annealer's advantage, if any, also lives on rugged, heterogeneous, non self averaging landscapes, and dense financial QUBOs additionally pay a heavy embedding cost to fit the machine's sparse hardware graph. The paper's structural verdict, that market generated ensembles are self averaging and benign, is annealer agnostic bad news.

One more actor deserves its billing: the QUBO formulation itself. The specific relevance and redundancy construction this paper uses comes from a twenty twenty five feature selection line of work, which the same research group had previously implemented on I B M hardware. That matters for fairness: the construction was not invented to fail; it is the community's own preferred encoding, taken as found, with its parameters calibrated by the paper's frozen procedure.

So the map is drawn: exponential algorithms that need machines nobody has, quadratic algorithms whose constants must face vectorized classical arithmetic, variational algorithms fighting their own training loops, and one noise robust sampler with real but young evidence. With that map in hand, let us pick up the classical tools the case studies will use as the measuring stick.
