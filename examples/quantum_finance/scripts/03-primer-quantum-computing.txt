Before we get to the three case studies, I need to give you two primers: one on quantum computing, one on the classical tools the paper uses as competition. Bear with me; every minute here pays off later.

First, quantum computing in five minutes. A normal computer stores information in bits, each of which is zero or one. A quantum computer stores information in qubits. A qubit can be in a blend of zero and one at the same time, a condition called superposition. The blend is described by two numbers called amplitudes, one attached to zero and one attached to one. Amplitudes are like probabilities with one crucial difference: they can be negative, or even complex numbers. When you measure a qubit, you get a definite zero or one, with probability equal to the square of the corresponding amplitude, and the superposition is destroyed.

Why does that help with anything? Because amplitudes can cancel. If two different computational paths lead to the same wrong answer, and one path carries a positive amplitude while the other carries a negative amplitude, they annihilate, and the wrong answer becomes less likely to appear when you measure. This is called interference, and it is the actual source of every quantum speedup. The popular image of a quantum computer trying all answers in parallel is misleading. It does, in a sense, explore many possibilities in superposition, but you only get one measured answer out, so the art of quantum algorithm design is choreographing interference so that wrong answers cancel and right answers reinforce. Keep that in mind: quantum computing is interference engineering, not magic parallelism.

Two more facts you need. First, with n qubits, the superposition lives over all two to the power n bit strings at once, which is why people get excited: thirty qubits already span about a billion configurations. Second, today's quantum hardware is noisy. Every operation has a small chance of error, and errors compound. The long-term fix is called fault tolerance or error correction: you bundle many physical qubits into one logical qubit that resists errors. The overhead is brutal. As you will hear later, one of the paper's calculations involves a machine with two hundred eighty two logical qubits that requires roughly eight hundred thousand physical qubits. That factor of several thousand is the price of reliability, and any honest feasibility study must include it.
