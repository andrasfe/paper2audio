To appreciate the measurements you are about to hear in case study two, you need the amplitude estimation machinery at full resolution: the geometry that produces the quadratic speedup, the iterative schedule that harvests it, the circuit constructions block by block, and, most importantly, the handful of experimental decisions without which the headline numbers would be artifacts. Linear algebra on, here we go.

Start from the abstract statement. Suppose a unitary A acts on a register of qubits initialized to all zeros and produces a state of the form: square root of one minus a, times a ket we will call bad, tensor a zero on a marker qubit, plus square root of a, times a ket called good, tensor a one on the marker. The number a between zero and one is the amplitude squared we want, in our application the tail probability of the loss distribution. Define an angle theta by a equals sine squared theta. Everything that follows is two dimensional geometry: the entire dynamics happens in the plane spanned by the bad and good vectors.

Build the Grover iterate: Q equals, A, times the reflection about the all zeros state, times A inverse, times the reflection that flips the sign of the good component. Each reflection is a matrix with eigenvalues plus and minus one; the product of two reflections in a plane is a rotation, and a short computation shows Q rotates the plane by exactly two theta. Therefore, after k applications of Q to the initial state, the state's angle is two k plus one, times theta, and measuring the marker returns one with probability sine squared of, two k plus one, theta. That formula is the whole engine. With k equals zero you are doing plain Monte Carlo: probability sine squared theta, and estimating theta to precision epsilon costs one over epsilon squared samples, the classical law. But at Grover power k, the unknown theta enters multiplied by two k plus one: the measurement statistics oscillate two k plus one times faster as a function of theta, so each shot carries roughly two k plus one squared times more statistical information about theta. Distribute your shot budget over geometrically growing powers k, and the total number of A applications needed for precision epsilon drops to order one over epsilon. Nothing about this argument is heuristic; it saturates the quantum Cramer Rao bound, which is why the speedup is provably optimal.

The iterative variant the paper runs, I Q A E, converts the geometry into an adaptive interval halving. Maintain a confidence interval for theta, initially the whole quarter circle. At each round, choose the largest power k such that the image of the current interval under multiplication by two k plus one still fits inside a half period of the sine squared function, so the mapping from measurement probability back to theta is single valued. Fire a batch of shots at that power, form a binomial confidence bound using Chernoff Hoeffding inequalities, pull the bound back through the sine, and shrink the theta interval. Repeat, k roughly doubling as the interval shrinks, until the interval is narrower than the target. The worst case total query count is one point four over epsilon, times a doubly logarithmic confidence factor, the exact expression the paper's resource counts use.

Now the experimental decision that, in my view, is the most instructive in the entire program, because getting it wrong produced a beautiful, wrong result first. The verification experiment measures how query count scales with target precision, expecting slope minus one on log log axes. The first configuration used a large, comfortable number of shots per round, over thirty thousand. The measured slope came out almost exactly zero. Think about why: with that many shots, the very first round's binomial interval was already so tight that the schedule terminated after one or two rounds for every target precision in the sweep; the algorithm was saturating, precision limited by the schedule floor rather than by the query budget, so cost did not grow as targets tightened. Zero slope did not mean magic; it meant the instrument was pegged. The documented fix: cut to one hundred twenty eight shots per round, so the adaptive schedule genuinely engages across the sweep, and, crucially, pre register a two sided acceptance gate on the fitted slope, it must land between minus one point six and minus zero point five. Two sided is the point: a slope near zero reveals saturation, a slope near minus two would reveal the estimator degenerating to classical sampling; either failure mode invalidates the run. The measured slope, minus one point zero three, passed inside a gate designed to catch both ways of fooling yourself. When someone shows you a scaling plot, ask what gate its slope had to pass; now you know why.

Next, the oracle constructions, because their costs decide the verdict. The state loader implements a probability distribution as amplitudes using the conditional probability tree, sometimes called the Grover Rudolph construction. Qubit one gets a Y rotation whose angle encodes the probability that the sample lies in the upper half of the grid; qubit two gets a rotation conditioned on qubit one, encoding the quarter within the half; and so on down m levels. Summing the tree, an m qubit loader costs two to the m minus two controlled rotations, each an arbitrary angle. On a fault tolerant machine every arbitrary rotation must be synthesized from Clifford and T gates at roughly three T per digit of accuracy, and the paper's convention fixes the synthesis accuracy at one tenth of the amplitude tolerance, a pre registered choice that prevents synthesis error from silently eating the error budget. Multiply it out across the factor register at seven qubits and forty nine idiosyncratic registers at five qubits, and you see mechanically why loading is seventy four percent of all T gates: exponentially many rotations per register is the constitutional weakness of amplitude encoding, priced here rather than hidden.

The arithmetic is friendlier. The weighted adder accumulates the portfolio loss: for each input bit of each register, conditionally add a fixed constant, that bit's weight, into a sum register whose width is the ceiling of log two of the maximum possible sum, about two Toffoli gates per sum bit per input bit using ripple carry addition. The comparator uses the twos complement trick: to ask whether the loss exceeds a threshold, add the constant, two to the sum width minus the threshold, and read the overflow bit; the overflow is exactly the comparison, for two Toffoli per sum bit including uncomputation. For expected shortfall, one more block: conditioned on the comparator flag, a payoff rotation writes the excess loss onto the marker amplitude, using the small angle linearization, sine of a small angle approximately equals the angle, so that the marker probability is linear in the conditional expectation; the linearization bias is controlled by keeping the rotation scale small, and the estimate maps back through, C Var equals VaR plus payoff scale times amplitude over one minus the confidence level. One quiet decision hides here: the VaR threshold itself is computed classically on the discretized model, rather than by a nested quantum bisection, because bisecting the threshold through repeated Q A E runs would multiply the query bill several fold for no informational gain; the pre registration commits to estimating the tail average beyond a known threshold, which is the workload a desk actually re-computes daily.

Verification architecture, briefly, because its design choices carry the credibility. The eighteen qubit instance is not a cartoon: it is the identical block structure, loader, adder, comparator, at truncated register widths, compiled to elementary gates, one thousand fifty two for one application of A, five thousand five hundred nine per Grover iterate. Its exact amplitude, zero point one three zero seven three seven, comes from brute force enumeration of the circuit, which is the referee no statistical test can argue with. And the register widths for the full scale counts were themselves gated: the discretization gate demands that halving the grid twice, a four times finer grid, moves the answer by less than half the tightest target, which fixed seven and five qubits as sufficient and, importantly, prevents the resource count from being flattered by an unrealistically coarse grid. Finally, the two independent counting methods: the analytic formula count, and a constructed circuit count from an actual compiled adder and comparator. They disagreed by twenty five percent against a pre registered twenty percent gate; the discrepancy was traced to the compiler's fatter generic decomposition, and the frozen resolution was to use the smaller count everywhere. Every one of these choices bends the same direction, in quantum's favor, which is what lets the final verdict, five orders of magnitude short, stand without an asterisk.
