Before I close, the shortcomings, stated as plainly as the paper states them, plus a few it does not.

First, everything quantum here is simulated or counted, never run on hardware. That was deliberate, zero quantum processor seconds were consumed, and it is what makes the measurements exact, but it means device-level surprises, good or bad, are out of frame, and the fault-tolerant projections inherit every assumption of the surface-code cost model and its hardware corners.

Second, size limits. The exact quantum M C M C analysis stops at twelve switches; Q A O A angle training happens on small sub-instances; the certified hard financial family starts around thirty two. The paper is admirably explicit that its case-three verdict is scale mismatch, not refutation, but the mismatch is real, and the bridging experiment at sizes sixteen to thirty two remains to be done.

Third, breadth of encodings. One QUBO construction, relevance and redundancy with a soft cardinality penalty, one data family, the French portfolios, and two dependence measures. The self-averaging conclusion plausibly generalizes, since the market-factor mechanism is universal in equities, but other asset classes, other objective designs, and deliberately adversarial constructions were not tested. It remains possible that some financial optimization problem, somewhere, is genuinely glassy; the paper shows the standard one is not.

Fourth, Q A O A was tested at shallow depth, two layers, with the fixed-angle deployment story as the target. That is the fair reading of current practice, and per-instance angle training was measured and found to scale worse, but a future breakthrough in angle-setting strategies is the kind of unknown no empirical study can foreclose.

Fifth, in the nested-simulation cells, the verdict turned out to be dominated by an accounting convention, the inner repricing tolerance, and the authors stopped at two conventions rather than resolving which one the industry should use. That is honest, but it leaves the most quantum-favorable corner of the amplitude estimation landscape, X V A style nested risk, without a definitive answer.

And sixth, a limitation of framing: classical baselines here are strong but conventional, exact solvers, simulated annealing, vectorized Monte Carlo. Specialized classical heuristics, or machine-learned proposal distributions for M C M C, could shift the goalposts further against quantum, meaning the paper's negative verdicts are, if anything, conservative.

So, to land the plane. This paper takes the three most cited quantum finance stories, subjects each to the full discipline of pre-registration, certified baselines, adversarial controls, and honesty clauses, on a century of real market data, and returns a differentiated verdict. Portfolio optimization by Q A O A: structurally unsuitable, because markets, especially stressed markets, produce self-averaging, homogeneous, classically easy problem ensembles, and the one regime with residual hardness is precisely where the quantum deployment protocol fails. Amplitude estimation for risk: the speedup is real, observed, and provably optimal, but at real desk precision, classical vectorized Monte Carlo wins by a hundred thousand fold, and the crossover sits at a precision no one needs; the derived criterion tells you exactly what kind of expensive-per-scenario workload could change that. Quantum-enhanced M C M C: passes its controls with a seven-fold exponent advantage on cold rugged landscapes, the real deal at small scale, but the financial instances small enough to verify are not rugged, so its financial case awaits the bridging experiment at larger sizes. And across all three, a portable lesson: before believing any quantum application claim, ask where the hardness lives, what the per-operation cost ratio is, and whether the validated scales overlap the hard scales. Those three questions cost almost nothing to answer and would have correctly predicted every verdict in this program.

That is the paper. Thanks for listening, and enjoy the rest of your flight.
