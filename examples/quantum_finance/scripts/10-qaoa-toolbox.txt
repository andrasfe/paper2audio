Time to open the Q A O A toolbox at full mathematical resolution. You have the physics; this chapter gives you the linear algebra, the exact objective the experiments optimized, the symmetries that had to be handled, and the design decisions, each of which turns out to be essential to the findings. If you know what an eigenvalue is, you can follow every step.

Set up the space first. For n binary variables, the quantum state lives in a vector space of dimension two to the n, with one basis vector, written as a ket, for each bit string z. A general state is a superposition, a linear combination of these basis kets with complex coefficients, and measuring returns string z with probability equal to the squared magnitude of its coefficient. Operators are matrices on this space. Two single qubit matrices generate everything we need: Z, which is diagonal with entries plus one and minus one, so it reads a bit; and X, the flip matrix, which swaps zero and one.

Step one: turn the QUBO into a Hamiltonian. Substitute, for each binary variable, x equals one half of, one minus Z. Push that substitution through the quadratic form x transpose Q x and collect terms: you get a constant, plus a sum of single Z terms with coefficients built from Q's diagonal and row sums, plus a sum of Z Z products over pairs, with coefficients proportional to the off diagonal couplings. The result, the cost Hamiltonian H C, is a diagonal matrix whose entry at basis state z is exactly the classical cost of portfolio z. Nothing quantum has happened yet; we have merely written the objective function on the diagonal of a two to the n dimensional matrix.

Step two: the circuit as matrix exponentials. Initialize in the uniform superposition, every coefficient equal to one over square root of two to the n, prepared by one layer of Hadamards. The cost layer is the unitary e to the minus i gamma H C. Because H C is diagonal, this multiplies each basis state's coefficient by a phase, e to the minus i gamma times its cost: cheap states rotate a little, expensive states rotate a lot. Implementation wise it decomposes into one two qubit Z Z rotation per nonzero coupling, which for these dense financial matrices means a full quadratic layer, worth remembering when anyone quotes circuit depths. The mixer layer is e to the minus i beta times the sum of all X operators, which factorizes into independent single qubit rotations, one per qubit. At depth p equals two, the prepared state is: mixer of beta two, cost of gamma two, mixer of beta one, cost of gamma one, applied to the uniform state; four angles.

Step three, and here is the first critical experimental decision: what number do you actually optimize and report? Two candidates exist in the literature. The common one is the energy expectation, the average cost under the output distribution, often normalized as an approximation ratio. This program deliberately rejected it, for a reason specific to financial QUBOs: their spectra are nearly degenerate near the bottom, dozens of portfolios within a fraction of a percent of optimal, so an algorithm can score a superb approximation ratio while essentially never producing the true optimum. Instead the frozen metric is ground state probability: p opt equals the squared magnitude of the overlap between the prepared state and the certified optimal string, and its operational form, shots to solution, one over p opt, the expected number of circuit repetitions until the optimum is actually observed. This choice is what makes the quantum and classical columns commensurable, because the classical metric, time to solution at ninety nine percent, is also an exact hit criterion against the same certified optimum. One metric definition, applied to both sides; change it, and none of the exponent comparisons would mean anything.

Step four: training, and its honest cost accounting. For each sub instance, the four angles are optimized against p opt using exact statevector simulation: the full two to the n coefficient vector is held in memory, each circuit application is a sequence of sparse matrix multiplications, and p opt is read off exactly, no sampling noise. The optimizer is a multi start search, coarse grid then local refinement, because the angle landscape is multimodal. Note the direction of the gift: on hardware, every optimizer evaluation would cost thousands of shots and inherit noise; in exact simulation the training is free and perfect. So measured training outcomes here are an upper bound on what hardware training could achieve, and the study's negative transfer results survive the objection automatically.

Step five: the symmetries, a small thing that was nearly a large mistake. The map from angles to output probabilities is not injective; it has exact symmetries, and if you ignore them, statistics about angle scatter are corrupted. Symmetry one: complex conjugation. Because H C and the mixer are real matrices, negating every gamma and every beta conjugates the state and leaves all probabilities unchanged; so plus and minus an angle vector are the same physical point. Symmetry two: each beta is periodic with period pi, up to irrelevant global phase, since the mixer rotations wrap. The pre registered fix, adopted before any regime data existed: canonicalize every trained angle vector, force the sign of the first gamma non negative and reduce betas modulo pi, then compute the circular standard deviation. Without the gauge fix, the same physical optimum appearing with both sign conventions would masquerade as maximal scatter, and the entire concentration screen, the study's central quantum measurement, would read noise.

Step six, the theory that makes concentration a meaningful diagnostic at all, and this is where linear algebra earns its keep. Why should optimal angles agree across instances of an ensemble? The trained objective, p opt or the energy, is built from expectation values of local terms, and at fixed angles each Z Z term's expectation depends only on a bounded neighborhood of couplings around that pair. Sum over pairs, and for ensembles whose couplings are drawn independently from a fixed distribution, the law of large numbers takes over: the angle landscape of a single instance converges, as n grows, to the ensemble average landscape, with fluctuations shrinking like one over square root of the instance size. The landscape freezes; its argmax freezes; every instance wants the same angles. That is the rigorous backbone of the fixed angle protocol, proved for structured cases like the Sherrington Kirkpatrick model, where optimal angles are literally universal constants. Now invert the logic for financial data: there, the coupling distribution itself is the random object, its mean and spread drift window to window with the market factor, so each window's landscape sits somewhere else, and trained angles track the drift. This is exactly why the paper's concentration statistic and its coupling coefficient of variation move together, and why sigma tightening in crises, when the coupling histogram compresses, is not a coincidence but a theorem playing out in data.

Step seven: the transfer protocol, made precise, because its failure is the single most decisive number in case study one. Definition: train angles on sub instances at sizes sixteen and eighteen; take the component wise median angle vector; freeze it; evaluate shots to solution at size twenty two with the frozen angles; divide by shots to solution at twenty two with per instance directly trained angles. That ratio is the price of deploying without training. The pre registered admissibility bar was three: fixed angles may cost up to three times more shots and the protocol still counts as viable, since it saves the entire training bill. Measured: fifty two point six and one hundred twenty two point four on the two calm windows. Not a marginal miss; a categorical one. And notice the design subtlety: the ratio is measured across sizes, sixteen and eighteen into twenty two, not within one size, because deployment in the real world means training small and running large. Concentration within a size had passed; the across size gate is the one that matters economically, and it is the one that failed.

Step eight: the scaling fits and their fine print. For each size n in the ladder, take the median of shots to solution across seeds, take base two logarithms, fit a line by least squares; the slope is the exponent. Same procedure for the classical annealer's time to solution, same sizes, same seeds, same certified optima. Two fine print items are load bearing. First, medians, not means: shots to solution is heavy tailed across seeds, and a single unlucky instance would otherwise own the fit. Second, the capped value pathology: when an instance is so easy or so degenerate that the solver saturates the measurement budget, its value enters as a bound, not a number, and fitting bounds as numbers biases slopes; this is precisely the artifact the paper flags in the crisis window annealing exponents and refuses to interpret. Reading exponent tables without asking who capped what is how this literature generates phantom advantages.

That is the toolbox: the diagonal Hamiltonian, the two exponential layers, the exact hit metric matched across quantum and classical, gauge fixed angle statistics, the concentration theorem and its data driven failure mode, the across size transfer ratio, and disciplined exponent fitting. Now watch every piece of it operate, in the study that ran one hundred fourteen cells of market data through this machinery.
