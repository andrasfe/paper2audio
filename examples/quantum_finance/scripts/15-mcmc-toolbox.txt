Case study three is moments away. To hear it at full resolution, open the sampling toolbox: the spectral theory that turns mixing speed into an eigenvalue problem, the exact construction of the quantum proposal, and the four or five design decisions, temperature scaling above all, that the study's first version got wrong and its second version got right. This chapter is the most linear algebraic of the course, and the payoff is that case study three's plots will read like sheet music.

A Markov chain on the two to the n configurations is a stochastic matrix P: row x lists the probabilities of stepping from x to each y, rows summing to one. The target is the Boltzmann distribution pi, probability of x proportional to e to the minus energy of x over T. The chain is built to satisfy detailed balance: pi of x, times P from x to y, equals pi of y, times P from y to x, for every pair; summing that identity over x shows pi is stationary, and the construction that enforces it is Metropolis: propose y from x with a symmetric proposal probability, accept with probability the minimum of one and e to the minus energy increase over T; put all rejected mass on the diagonal. Symmetry of the proposal is what makes the acceptance formula this simple, because the proposal ratio cancels out of the detailed balance condition; hold that thought for the quantum step.

Now the spectral machinery. Detailed balance is exactly the statement that P is self adjoint with respect to the pi weighted inner product; concretely, conjugating by the diagonal square root of pi, S equals D to the half, P, D to the minus half, produces a genuinely symmetric matrix with the same eigenvalues. So the spectrum is real, lives in the interval minus one to plus one, and has a single eigenvalue at plus one whose eigenvector encodes pi. Every initial distribution's distance from equilibrium decays as the powers of the subdominant eigenvalues, so convergence speed is governed by the absolute spectral gap: one minus the larger of, the second eigenvalue, and the absolute value of the most negative eigenvalue. The most negative one matters because an eigenvalue near minus one means period two sloshing, oscillation that also refuses to die. Standard bounds sandwich the mixing time between one over the gap and, one over the gap times the log of one over the smallest stationary probability, so measuring the gap is measuring relaxation, up to logarithms that do not move exponents. That is why the study's cost metric is simply log base two of one over the gap, and why its slopes against n are relaxation exponents.

The decision to compute gaps exactly, rather than estimate mixing from running chains, is the study's foundational trade. Exact means: build the full two to the n by two to the n transition matrix, symmetrize as above, and take the complete eigendecomposition, no sampling error, no burn in ambiguity, detailed balance checkable to machine precision, and it was checked, residuals below ten to the minus fifteen everywhere. The price is brutal scaling: dense eigendecomposition costs on the order of the matrix dimension cubed, so each added spin multiplies the work by eight, which walls the method at twelve to fourteen spins. The study accepted the wall knowingly, because at these sizes every number is beyond dispute; the cost, as you will hear, is that the wall sits below where financial hardness begins. One implementation decision inside this choice deserves its war story: the first attempt used sparse iterative eigensolvers, which are the standard tool when you only need a few eigenvalues. At cold temperature they stalled catastrophically, one instance sat for fifty seven minutes, because metastability is spectral clustering: each deep valley contributes an eigenvalue exponentially close to one, and iterative methods converge at a rate set by the very gaps that are collapsing. The dense solver, immune to clustering, became the frozen choice. When your observable is a nearly closed gap, do not use an algorithm whose runtime diverges as gaps close.

Now the quantum proposal, precisely. The Hamiltonian is a weighted blend: one minus gamma, times alpha, times the problem Hamiltonian, the diagonal matrix of energies, plus gamma times the transverse field, the sum of X operators, whose off diagonal pattern connects every configuration to its n single flip neighbors. The normalizer alpha is the square root of, n divided by, the sum of squared couplings plus squared fields; its job is dimensional hygiene, scaling the problem term so its operator magnitude is comparable to the mixer's regardless of instance, which is what makes a single gamma range, a quarter to zero point six, meaningful across ensembles. The proposal is: evolve for time t under this Hamiltonian, then measure in the computational basis; the probability of proposing y from x is the squared magnitude of the y x entry of e to the minus i H t. Two structural facts, both one line proofs, carry the algorithm. Fact one, symmetry: H is a real symmetric matrix, so its matrix exponential is a symmetric, though complex, matrix, hence the y x and x y entries are equal, hence their squared magnitudes are equal: the proposal matrix is exactly symmetric, and the simple Metropolis acceptance is valid with no correction term. This is the noise robustness theorem in miniature: any real symmetric H, including one distorted by hardware imperfections, yields a legal proposal; errors reshape which moves get proposed, never the distribution being sampled. Fact two, unitarity: the rows of the proposal matrix sum to one automatically, each row of a unitary has unit norm, so the proposal is doubly stochastic before Metropolis even touches it. Why does such a proposal help? Perturbation intuition: at moderate gamma the eigenvectors of H are computational states hybridized mainly with configurations of nearby energy, so the evolution transfers amplitude preferentially between near degenerate configurations, valley floor to valley floor, and those long range, energy conserving moves are accepted at cold temperature exactly where single flips are being rejected. The study draws gamma uniformly and the evolution time uniformly between two and twenty, and averages the proposal over six independent draws, a pre registered randomization that prevents accidental fine tuning to resonances of particular instances, mirroring the reference experiment's protocol.

And then, temperature, the decision that broke version one and defines the lesson of the study. Version one set each instance's temperature ladder from the median magnitude of its own single flip energy changes, a seemingly innocent adaptive choice. Do the linear algebra: flipping spin i changes the energy by twice the magnitude of, its field plus its row of couplings dotted into the current configuration, and for dense instances that row dot product is a sum of n independent terms, so its typical magnitude grows like the square root of n times the root mean square coupling. The adaptive ladder therefore silently heated larger instances by a square root of n factor, flattening classical slopes, steepening quantum ones, and the positive control gate caught the corruption: quantum slope worse than single flip on spin glasses, physically impossible if the pipeline were sound. The amendment defines the fix in one word: intensive. Temperature must be measured in units of an intensive coupling scale, one that does not grow with system size; version two uses the root mean square coupling itself, with rungs at that scale, half it, and a quarter of it. On the control ensemble, couplings are additionally drawn with the Sherrington Kirkpatrick normalization, scaled by two over the square root of n, precisely so that energies per spin and the intensive temperature ladder remain comparable across sizes. Every scaling claim in case study three lives downstream of this correction, and the honest sequence, gate failure, diagnosis, amendment, full re run, is why the corrected numbers deserve belief.

Last pieces of fine print, quickly. Fits are lines through medians of log inverse gaps across seeds at each size, slopes with standard errors from residuals, and the pre registered win rule demands both a three quarters slope ratio and separation beyond the summed standard errors, at two of three temperatures, a deliberately conservative compound test for a study whose largest matrices allow only seven sizes. And size six was added in version two purely to stabilize three point fits, a fragility confession worth copying.

Toolbox closed, sheet music in hand. Now case study three: fifty seven instances, four proposals, one failed gate, one decisive control, and a null exactly where the money wanted a signal.
