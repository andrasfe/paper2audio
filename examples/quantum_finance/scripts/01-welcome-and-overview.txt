Over the next roughly fifty minutes I am going to walk you through a research paper titled Quantum in Finance: Three Case Studies on the Feasibility of Quantum Computing for Financial Workloads. I will assume you know essentially nothing about quantum computing, nothing about the mathematics involved, and only a little about finance. Everything the paper uses, I will build up from scratch before it is needed. By the end you should understand not just what the paper found, but exactly how it found it, why the methods are trustworthy, what the contributions are, and where the honest weaknesses lie.

Here is the one-sentence version, so you know where we are going. The paper asks whether quantum computers could ever be genuinely useful for three classic financial jobs, tests each one carefully on a century of real market data, and finds that the most hyped application, portfolio optimization, fails for a deep structural reason, while two less hyped applications show real, measurable quantum promise that is nevertheless nowhere near practical today. The constructive product of the paper is a set of three measurable screening tests that any future quantum finance proposal should pass before anyone spends money on quantum hardware.
