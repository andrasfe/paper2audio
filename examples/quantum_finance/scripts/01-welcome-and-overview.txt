Welcome. This is a full length audio course, a bit over two hours, built around a research paper titled Quantum in Finance: Three Case Studies on the Feasibility of Quantum Computing for Financial Workloads. I will assume you know essentially nothing about quantum computing, nothing about the mathematics involved, and only a little about finance. Everything the paper uses, I will build from scratch before it is needed, and where the paper compresses, I will decompress: the actual equations read aloud in plain words, the implementation machinery behind every experiment, the physics of why the answers came out the way they did, and the objections a hostile expert would raise, with their answers.

Here is the one sentence version, so you know where we are going. The paper asks whether quantum computers could ever be genuinely useful for three classic financial jobs, tests each one under pre registered rules on a century of real market data, and finds that the most hyped application, portfolio optimization, fails for a deep structural reason, while two less hyped applications show real, measurable quantum promise that is nevertheless nowhere near practical today, and it distills the whole program into three cheap screening tests any future quantum finance proposal should pass before anyone spends money on hardware.

A quick map of the journey, in five movements. First, foundations: the feasibility question itself, how quantum computers work, gates, entanglement, error correction, and a tour of the quantum algorithm zoo that explains how finance ended up first in line. Second, the toolkit: the optimization problems called QUBOs, the classical solvers that set the bar, the data, and a masterclass in the pre registration methodology that makes these results trustworthy. Third, the heart of the course: the three case studies in painful detail, portfolio selection with Q A O A, tail risk with quantum amplitude estimation, and sampling with quantum enhanced Markov chain Monte Carlo, with two physics interludes, one on spin glasses and why market data refuses to produce them, and one inside the fault tolerant machine itself. Fourth, the adversarial chapters: ten objections, played honestly, and a research agenda with the next five experiments. Fifth, the honest accounting of limitations, and the closing argument. Every chapter stands on the ones before it, but each opens with enough of a bridge that you can pause anywhere and pick the thread back up. Settle in; here we go.
