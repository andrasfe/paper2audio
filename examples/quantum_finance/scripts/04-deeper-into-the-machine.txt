Before we leave the machine room, let us go one level deeper into how a quantum computer actually computes, because three ideas from this chapter, circuits built from gates, the cost of simulating them classically, and the machinery of error correction, will carry the entire second half of this talk.

Start with gates. A classical computer computes with logic gates, AND, OR, NOT, acting on bits. A quantum computer computes with quantum gates acting on qubits, and every quantum gate is, mathematically, a rotation. Remember that a single qubit's state is a pair of amplitudes; you can picture it as an arrow on a sphere, with north pole meaning zero and south pole meaning one. A single qubit gate rotates that arrow. Some rotations are gentle, some flip the arrow completely, and one gate you will meet again, called the T gate, rotates the arrow by exactly one eighth of a turn around the vertical axis. Two qubit gates are where the real power enters: the controlled not gate flips a target qubit only when a control qubit is one, but crucially, it does this while both qubits are in superposition, which knits their amplitudes together. After a controlled not, you generally cannot describe either qubit on its own anymore; only the pair, jointly, has a definite state. That condition is entanglement. A famous small example: put one qubit in an equal superposition, then apply a controlled not to a second qubit. Now the pair is in a superposition of both zero and both one, and nothing in between. Measure either qubit and you instantly know the other's outcome, no matter how the measurements are arranged. Entanglement is not a communication channel, it is a resource of correlation, and it is the raw material interference works with.

A quantum circuit is just a sequence of these gates applied to a register of qubits, drawn left to right like a musical score, ending in measurements. Circuit depth, the number of sequential layers, is the quantum analog of running time, and it matters doubly on real hardware because errors accumulate with every layer. A useful theorem to know: a small fixed set of gates is universal, meaning any quantum computation whatsoever can be compiled into just those gates. The set that matters for error corrected machines is the Clifford gates, which are cheap, plus that T gate, which is expensive, and that split will explain a great deal in case study two.

Now, why can you not just simulate all this on a laptop and skip the quantum computer? Sometimes you can, and this paper's whole methodology depends on it. The state of n qubits is a list of two to the power n complex amplitudes. Each complex number takes sixteen bytes at double precision. Twenty qubits: sixteen megabytes, trivial. Thirty qubits: seventeen gigabytes, a beefy workstation. Forty qubits: seventeen terabytes. Every added qubit doubles the memory, and every gate application sweeps the entire list. The paper lived this arithmetic directly: an early version of its amplitude estimation experiment accidentally specified a thirty three qubit circuit, which would have needed one hundred thirty one gigabytes of memory, and had to be cut down to eighteen qubits, a few megabytes, before it could run. This exponential wall is exactly why exact classical verification is confined to small instances, a constraint you will see shape case study three, and it is also, of course, the very reason quantum hardware might one day be worth building.

Next, noise, the villain of the era. Real qubits are physical objects, superconducting circuits chilled near absolute zero, or trapped ions held by lasers, and they interact with stray fields, vibrations, and each other. Three failure modes matter. Decoherence: a qubit's delicate phase relationship with the rest of the register leaks into the environment and is lost; today's best qubits hold coherence for microseconds to milliseconds. Gate error: every operation is an analog control pulse, and it lands slightly wrong, roughly one error in a thousand for two qubit gates on today's better machines. Measurement error: reading out a qubit misreports it maybe once in a hundred times. Multiply those out and an uncorrected computation is limited to a few hundred or thousand operations before it turns to mush. The current era is called NISQ, noisy intermediate scale quantum, and every NISQ result must be read with those error rates in mind.

The escape route is quantum error correction, and it has to clear a conceptual hurdle classical error correction never faced. Classically you protect data by copying it: store three copies, take majority votes. Quantum mechanics flatly forbids copying an unknown quantum state; that is the no cloning theorem. The solution is subtler: spread one logical qubit's worth of information across many physical qubits in an entangled pattern, and then measure only carefully chosen collective properties, called stabilizers or parity checks, that reveal whether an error occurred, and which one, without revealing, and hence without disturbing, the encoded information itself. Think of it as auditing a ledger by checking that rows and columns sum correctly, without ever reading the individual entries. A classical decoder algorithm watches the stream of parity check results, infers the most likely error, and the correction is applied. The headline scheme, the surface code, arranges physical qubits on a checkerboard and achieves something remarkable: as long as the physical error rate is below a threshold of roughly one percent, adding more physical qubits per logical qubit suppresses the logical error rate exponentially. Reliability becomes an engineering dial. The price is the overhead you have already heard: hundreds to thousands of physical qubits per logical qubit.

And there is a second price, less famous and more consequential for this paper. The surface code performs the cheap Clifford gates almost for free, but it cannot directly perform the T gate, the one eighth turn, and without T gates the computer is not universal; in fact, circuits made only of Clifford gates can be simulated efficiently on a classical computer, so all the quantum power hides in the T gates. The workaround is called magic state distillation: prepare many noisy copies of a special one qubit resource state, run them through a purification circuit, the standard recipe turns fifteen noisy copies into one much cleaner copy, and repeat in layers until the state is clean enough; then one clean magic state, consumed, enacts one T gate. Dedicated regions of the machine, T factories, do nothing but churn these out, and for most fault tolerant algorithms the factories set the speed limit: the computation proceeds exactly as fast as T gates can be supplied. That is why resource estimates, including this paper's, are quoted in T counts, and why an innocent looking step, rotating a qubit by an arbitrary angle, becomes costly: an arbitrary rotation must be approximated by a long sequence of Clifford and T gates, roughly three T gates per digit of accuracy, so a rotation accurate to one part in ten thousand costs about forty T gates. Hold that thought; in case study two, seventy four percent of the entire quantum bill will come from exactly this.

That is the machine, honestly priced. Next, let us survey the algorithms people run on it, and how finance, of all industries, ended up first in line at the door.
