Now, case study one: portfolio selection with the quantum approximate optimization algorithm, which I will call Q A O A from here on, spoken as its four letters.

The financial task first. Suppose you must pick, say, twelve assets out of a pool that jointly track the overall market while being minimally redundant with each other. Index tracking funds do this, hedge desks do this, and machine learning people do the same thing when selecting features. The natural encoding is a QUBO: each asset gets a switch, the individual terms reward assets that are relevant to the market factor, and the pairwise terms penalize pairs of assets that are highly dependent on each other, since holding both is wasteful. Dependence between assets is measured with statistics called distance correlation and mutual information; all you need to know is that they capture how strongly two return series move together, including in nonlinear ways. A soft penalty term enforces choosing about twelve assets. So the market data itself generates the optimization instances, and, crucially, the character of those instances changes with the market regime. That is what makes this a beautiful natural experiment.

Now the algorithm. Q A O A is the flagship quantum optimization algorithm. Here is how it works, gently. You prepare all qubits, one per asset switch, in an equal superposition of every possible portfolio. Then you alternate two operations, over and over. The first, called the cost layer, rotates the quantum phase of each configuration in proportion to its cost, so bad portfolios get tagged, in their phase, as bad. The second, called the mixer layer, stirs amplitude between configurations that differ by one switch flip. Phases are invisible to measurement on their own, but the mixer converts phase differences into amplitude differences, and if everything is tuned right, interference concentrates amplitude on low-cost configurations. After p rounds of this alternation you measure, and you hope to read out a near-optimal portfolio. The tuning knobs are two angles per round, called gamma and beta: gamma scales how strongly the cost layer tags, beta scales how strongly the mixer stirs. With p equals two rounds, you have four angles to tune.

Here is the catch that decides everything. Q A O A performance depends entirely on those angles, and finding good angles for a specific instance is itself a hard optimization problem that consumes many runs of the quantum device. If you have to pay that tuning cost per instance, the total bill usually erases any advantage. The practical hope of the field is the fixed-angle protocol: on structured families of problems, it has been observed that the optimal angles barely vary from instance to instance and even from size to size. If that holds, you train angles once on small, cheap instances and deploy them everywhere. The magic phrase is angle concentration: do the trained angles across many instances cluster tightly, or scatter widely? And this is measurable without any quantum computer, by simulating small instances classically, training angles, and computing the circular standard deviation of the results. Circular standard deviation is just the usual notion of spread, adapted to angles, which wrap around like a clock face; small sigma means tight clustering, and values around one or more, in radians, mean the angles are essentially all over the dial.

Now the hypothesis the study was actually designed to test. A widely voiced idea says: during market crises, correlations change so dramatically that the resulting QUBOs become rugged and classically hard, and that is exactly the opening a quantum optimizer needs. Crisis equals hardness equals quantum opportunity. The authors froze that hypothesis in the pre-registration and measured it across one hundred fourteen cells on the forty nine industry universe. A cell here means one combination of time window, dependence measure, and problem setting, run end to end.

The result is a clean reversal. Every single hardness indicator moved in the opposite direction from the hypothesis. Let me read you the headline table, which uses pools of thirty two assets choosing twelve. In calm two thousand six, the angle spread sigma was one point four seven, simulated annealing needed about one point six million steps to reliably hit the certified optimum, and the landscape held about three thousand nine hundred seventy local minima. Calm twenty nineteen: sigma one point zero four, about two point four million steps, about three thousand nine hundred seventy two local minima. Now the crises. The two thousand eight crisis window: sigma zero point four eight, three times tighter than calm, and simulated annealing needed only about one hundred thirty thousand steps, roughly twelve times faster. The covid window is the most dramatic: sigma zero point seven two, simulated annealing needed just two point four thousand steps, about a thousand times faster than calm, and the landscape had only eight hundred sixteen local minima, five times fewer. Crises did not harden these problems. Crises made them dramatically easier.

The paper's first figure shows this as a movie rather than snapshots. Along the horizontal axis runs calendar time through two periods, two thousand five to twenty ten and twenty eighteen to twenty one, with over a hundred overlapping half-year windows. The top panel plots the angle concentration; the bottom panel plots market volatility and the average pairwise correlation between industries. The two move in lockstep, mirror image: exactly when volatility spikes and correlations surge, in late two thousand eight and March twenty twenty, the angle spread collapses. The correlation between market correlation and the logarithm of classical solving time is about minus zero point six seven across the rolling windows. Tighter markets, easier problems, more concentrated angles, all together.

Why does this happen? This is where the controls earn their keep. The authors ran two scrambling experiments. In the first, they took a QUBO and randomly permuted its coupling values among the pairs, preserving the histogram of values but destroying which pair got which value. Hardness was preserved. In the second, they destroyed the cross-dependence structure in the underlying returns by shuffling time independently per asset, which flattens the histogram of couplings. Hardness vanished. Conclusion: the difficulty lives in the heterogeneity of the coupling distribution, meaning the spread of pairwise interaction strengths, not in any specific network arrangement. And that explains the reversal. In a crisis, everything crashes together; every asset's behavior is dominated by the single market factor; all pairwise dependencies converge toward the same value. Measured concretely, the coefficient of variation of the off-diagonal couplings, which is spread divided by mean, drops from zero point one seven in calm markets to zero point zero six in crisis. A crisis homogenizes the couplings, and homogeneous couplings make an easy landscape, the way a mountain range sanded down to uniform bumps is easy to descend.

The deepest concept in the paper enters here: self-averaging. An ensemble of random problem instances is self-averaging if, as instances get larger, they become more alike, so the typical instance represents them all, and fluctuations of, say, the optimal cost shrink with size. Spin glasses, the genuinely quantum-interesting problems, are famously not self-averaging: individual large instances stay idiosyncratic and pathological. The authors probed this directly, measuring how optimum-energy fluctuations scale with instance size, and found shrinkage everywhere: financial dependence QUBOs are self-averaging in every regime tested. There is no spin-glass signature. Statistically, typical financial instances are alike, and benign.

There is a final twist, which the paper calls the concentration-hardness trade-off, and it is the most elegant point in case study one. Remember the two things a quantum optimizer needs: hard instances, so classical methods struggle, and concentrated angles, so the fixed-angle deployment trick works. The study found that one single variable, coupling heterogeneity, controls both, with opposite signs. Calm markets give heterogeneous couplings: classically harder instances, but angle spread at the useless plateau around one to one point five, so no fixed-angle protocol. Crises give homogeneous couplings: beautifully concentrated angles, but instances that simulated annealing solves in milliseconds. Where Q A O A is deployable, it is not needed. Where it might be needed, it is not deployable. The market moves both levers at once, in the unhelpful directions.

Could raw Q A O A power overcome this? The scaling measurements say no. On matched sub-instances, the number of Q A O A measurement shots needed to hit the optimum grew like two to the power of about zero point eight seven to one point two five times n, where n is the number of assets. Simulated annealing on the same instances grew like two to the power of only zero point one four to zero point one six times n. Both are exponential, but the exponents differ by a factor of five to eight, and exponents compound mercilessly: every added asset multiplies Q A O A's cost by roughly two while multiplying simulated annealing's by roughly one point one.

The authors then stress-tested their own negative result by moving to the richer one hundred portfolio universe, where instances get genuinely harder: the exact certification method starts timing out at six hundred seconds in thirteen of sixteen cells, and simulated annealing fails to even find the calm-window optima on pools of one hundred, landing one point one to one point seven percent above the best known cost. Crisis windows stayed easy even here, confirming the regime effect at scale. On this harder family, an admissibility study found the most quantum-favorable trends in the whole program: angle concentration genuinely improved, sigma between zero point five seven and zero point eight nine, and the Q A O A to simulated annealing exponent ratio collapsed from five-to-eight-fold down to about one point three, with exponents of about one point zero versus about zero point seven eight. The paper is candid that this is the first trend pointing toward, rather than away from, an eventual crossover family. But the decisive gate failed anyway: for the fixed-angle protocol to work, angles trained on small instances must transfer to larger ones, and the pre-registered transfer test failed by a factor of fifty two to one hundred twenty two. Concentration held across instances of the same size but did not extend across sizes. Without transfer, every instance needs its own expensive training, and that is not a protocol, it is a tax.

Verdict, case study one, as frozen by the pre-registered rules: no case for Q A O A on financial dependence QUBOs. The residual hardness in calm windows is real, but classical annealing keeps a decisive scaling edge exactly where that hardness lives, and the deployment protocol Q A O A would need fails its gates. And the reason is structural, not an engineering shortfall: these ensembles are self-averaging and homogenize under stress.
