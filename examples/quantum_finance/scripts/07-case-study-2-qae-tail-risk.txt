Let us move to the second case study: quantum amplitude estimation for tail risk. Here the story arc is completely different: the quantum speedup is real and directly observed, and the workload still loses, for a reason worth understanding precisely.

Background first. Banks measure risk with two standard numbers. Value at risk answers: what loss threshold will be exceeded only one percent of the time over one day. Expected shortfall, also called C-Var, answers the follow-up: on those worst one percent of days, how big is the average loss. Regulators require these numbers; the industry computes them by Monte Carlo simulation, which means: simulate the portfolio under many random market scenarios, look at the loss distribution, read off the tail. Monte Carlo has a well known statistical law: to make your estimate twice as precise, you need four times as many scenarios. Error shrinks like one over the square root of the number of samples, so cost grows like one over the error squared. Halve the error, quadruple the bill.

Quantum amplitude estimation, which I will call Q A E, attacks exactly that law. Here is the idea, built from what you already know. Suppose you can build a quantum circuit that encodes the market scenario distribution into a superposition, computes the portfolio loss for every scenario in that superposition, and flips a marker qubit on the scenarios where the loss exceeds a threshold. Then the probability that the marker reads one equals precisely the tail probability you want, and that probability is the square of an amplitude sitting in the quantum state. Naively you would measure many times and count, which is just Monte Carlo again. The trick is a technique descended from Grover's search algorithm: you can construct an operation that rotates the quantum state, in a two dimensional plane, by an angle proportional to the amplitude you are trying to learn. Apply it once, twice, four times, and the tiny amplitude gets amplified in a controlled, readable way. By scheduling these applications cleverly and combining the measurement statistics, you learn the amplitude to error epsilon using a number of circuit runs proportional to one over epsilon, not one over epsilon squared. That is a quadratic speedup, and unlike almost every other quantum speedup you hear about, this one is unconditional and provably optimal. It does not rest on unproven conjectures. The paper uses the modern iterative variant, I Q A E, which achieves this without the expensive extra machinery of textbook phase estimation.

The workload the authors chose: compute the ninety nine percent one-day expected shortfall of an equal-weight portfolio of the forty nine industries, to accuracies of ten, two, and one half basis points. A basis point is one hundredth of one percent, the natural unit of precision on a risk desk. And now the discipline kicks in, because before comparing anything they verified every layer, with gates.

Layer one, the market model. They fitted a one-factor Student t model to the French data. Translation: each industry's return is driven by a shared market factor plus its own noise, and the randomness uses a Student t distribution, a bell curve with heavy tails, tail heaviness parameter nu around four point seven, meaning extreme days are far more likely than a Gaussian would say, which matches real markets. The model's value at risk was then backtested out of sample on twenty twenty one through twenty twenty five data using the Kupiec coverage test, a standard statistical check that the predicted one percent threshold really gets breached about one percent of the time. It passed comfortably, with a p value of zero point three five. So the quantum machine is being pointed at a model that demonstrably works, not a toy.

Layer two, the discretization. A quantum register can only hold finitely many scenario values, so the continuous distribution must be put on a grid. Too coarse a grid biases the answer. They imposed an honesty gate: the grid bias, measured against a four times finer grid, must stay below half the target accuracy. Register sizes were chosen to pass that gate.

Layer three, the oracle, meaning the actual quantum circuit: state-loading circuitry for the market distribution, fixed-point arithmetic to add up the portfolio loss, and a comparator to flip the marker qubit past the threshold. They built it, at a truncated size fitting in eighteen qubits, and simulated it end to end, exactly, on a classical machine, running the full I Q A E procedure against exact enumeration of the same circuit. Every estimate landed inside its confidence interval. And here is the headline: across the accuracy ladder, the measured cost scaling was epsilon to the minus one point zero three for the quantum procedure versus epsilon to the minus two point zero zero for classical Monte Carlo on the same model. Theory says exponents one and two; measurement says one point zero three and two point zero zero. The quadratic speedup is not an assumption in this paper. It was observed directly, on a data-calibrated oracle, and at a target error of ten to the minus three the quantum procedure was already using six times fewer oracle calls than Monte Carlo used samples.

So Q A E wins? No, and the reason is the most instructive number in the paper. Counting circuit resources, done two independent ways as a cross-check, with the twenty five percent discrepancy between the methods documented and the smaller count used, favoring quantum, and mapping onto a surface-code fault-tolerant machine at an optimistic hardware corner, physical error rate ten to the minus four, one hundred nanosecond cycles, one hundred parallel T factories, which are the modules that manufacture a special resource needed for quantum arithmetic, the full-calibration job at two basis points accuracy comes out to zero point four three hours on a machine with two hundred eighty two logical qubits, roughly eight hundred thousand physical qubits. The authors note the machine itself is surprisingly plausible, under a million physical qubits, the kind of scale hardware roadmaps target for the twenty thirties. But the same job on one classical processor core, measured, not estimated: zero point four seconds. On a fifty-times-parallel graphics card: eight milliseconds. Classical wins by around five orders of magnitude, and the pattern repeats across the calm and covid calibration windows, at zero point one nine hours versus zero point one six seconds, and zero point nine six hours versus two point eight seconds respectively.

How can a genuine quadratic speedup lose this badly? Because a speedup in the number of operations says nothing about the cost per operation. One classical Monte Carlo sample here is a few nanoseconds of vectorized arithmetic. One quantum oracle call is an error-corrected circuit costing enormously more; the measured ratio of per-call to per-sample cost in this study is ten to the eighth to ten to the ninth. A quadratic advantage in counts must overcome a hundred-million-fold handicap per count. Since quantum cost grows like one over epsilon and classical like one over epsilon squared, the classical curve is steeper, and the two lines do cross. The frontier figure in the paper plots both costs against target accuracy on logarithmic axes: the quantum band, spanning hardware corners, sits far above the classical line at practical accuracies, converging slowly as accuracy tightens. The crossover accuracy epsilon star lands near ten to the minus nine, five orders of magnitude finer than any real risk desk needs. Nobody prices risk to a billionth.

Out of this the paper distills its single equation, the workload-selection criterion, which I will read as a sentence. Quantum amplitude estimation beats classical Monte Carlo on wall-clock only when the required accuracy epsilon is finer than sigma times the classical per-sample cost, divided by kappa times the quantum per-oracle-call cost, where sigma is the standard deviation of the quantity being averaged and kappa collects the modest constant factors of the quantum schedule. Every symbol is measurable today for any proposed workload. That turns quantum feasibility hype into a five-minute arithmetic screen.

The criterion also says exactly what a winnable workload must look like: the classical per-sample cost must be huge, so the handicap shrinks. The natural candidate is nested simulation, where valuing one scenario itself requires an entire inner Monte Carlo run, as in X V A calculations, the derivative-portfolio adjustments that banks agonize over, where each outer scenario requires repricing a whole book of derivatives. The authors tested two such nested cells on option books. Neither cleared the criterion, and the failure modes are instructive. In the first cell, the inner repricing of pooled European basket options turned out to be statistically easy: a couple of hundred to a few thousand inner samples suffice, so the per-scenario cost never gets large enough. The second cell, with more exotic barrier options and a different tolerance convention, showed the opposite behavior, and together the pair demonstrated that the verdict is dominated by the inner pricing-tolerance convention, meaning how you specify required repricing accuracy across a book of options with wildly different premiums. The authors flag this openly and, notably, refused to shop for a third convention that might have produced the answer they wanted, since that would be the forking-paths disease. The verdict as frozen: the speedup is real; standard risk workloads are about ten to the fifth times too cheap per scenario for it to matter; a viable Q A E application must first demonstrate expensive, statistically hard inner valuation under a pre-committed tolerance schedule.
