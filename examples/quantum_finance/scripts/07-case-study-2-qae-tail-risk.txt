Let us move to the second case study: quantum amplitude estimation for tail risk. Here the story arc is completely different: the quantum speedup is real and directly observed, and the workload still loses, for a reason worth understanding precisely. As with case study one, I am going to take you through the full machinery: the market model equation by equation, the quantum circuit block by block, the resource counting formula by formula, and the nested simulation follow up that sharpened the conclusion.

Background first. Banks measure risk with two standard numbers. Value at risk answers: what loss threshold will be exceeded only one percent of the time over one day. Expected shortfall, also called C-Var, answers the follow up: on those worst one percent of days, how big is the average loss. Regulators require these numbers; the industry computes them by Monte Carlo simulation: simulate the portfolio under many random market scenarios, look at the loss distribution, read off the tail. Monte Carlo has a well known statistical law: error shrinks like one over the square root of the number of samples, so cost grows like one over the error squared. Halve the error, quadruple the bill.

Quantum amplitude estimation, Q A E, attacks exactly that law. Suppose you can build a quantum circuit that encodes the market scenario distribution into a superposition, computes the portfolio loss for every scenario in that superposition, and flips a marker qubit on the scenarios where the loss exceeds a threshold. Then the probability that the marker reads one equals precisely the tail probability you want, and that probability is the square of an amplitude in the quantum state. Naively you would measure many times and count, which is just Monte Carlo again. The trick descends from Grover's search algorithm: you can construct an operation that rotates the state, in a two dimensional plane, by an angle set by the amplitude you are trying to learn, and apply it one, two, four, many times, amplifying the tiny amplitude in a controlled, readable way. Scheduling those applications cleverly, you learn the amplitude to error epsilon with a number of circuit runs proportional to one over epsilon rather than one over epsilon squared. That quadratic speedup is unconditional and provably optimal; it rests on no conjectures. The paper uses the modern iterative variant, I Q A E, which avoids the extra machinery of textbook phase estimation, and its call count formula will appear shortly.

The workload: the ninety nine percent one day expected shortfall of the equal weight portfolio of the forty nine industries, to accuracies of ten, two, and one half basis points, where a basis point is one hundredth of one percent, the natural unit on a risk desk. And now the discipline kicks in, because before comparing anything, every layer is verified with a gate.

Layer one, the market model, and here is the actual equation. Each industry's daily return r i equals mu i, its average return, plus beta i times F, its exposure to a common market factor, plus s i times e i, its own idiosyncratic noise. The factor F is the standardized cross sectional market return; each beta comes from a linear regression of the asset on the factor; s i is the standard deviation of what the regression leaves over. The crucial modeling choice is that both the factor and the idiosyncratic noises follow Student t distributions, bell curves with heavy tails. The tail heaviness parameter, nu, is fitted by moment matching: for a Student t, the excess kurtosis, which measures how much more extreme the tails are than a Gaussian, equals six divided by nu minus four; invert that, and nu equals four plus six over the measured excess kurtosis, clamped between four point five and fifty for numerical sanity. On the French data the fit gives nu around four point six to four point seven, meaning genuinely heavy tails; a Gaussian model would be rejected instantly by any risk desk. Model adequacy is then tested out of sample with the Kupiec coverage test, and here is that formula too. Compute the model's ninety nine percent value at risk; count how many days in twenty twenty one through twenty twenty five the actual portfolio loss exceeded it; under a correct model that count is binomial with a one percent rate. The test statistic is minus two times the log likelihood ratio between the one percent model and the empirically observed violation rate, which follows a chi squared distribution with one degree of freedom. Measured: sixteen violations against twelve point six expected, p value zero point three five. The model is not rejected; the quantum machine will be pointed at a model that demonstrably works.

Layer two, discretization, because a quantum register holds finitely many values. The factor and each idiosyncratic term are put on grids spanning plus and minus six standard deviations, with two to the m points for an m qubit register; each grid point's probability is the difference of the Student t cumulative distribution function between neighboring grid edges, the standard truncated loading scheme. How many qubits are enough? A pre registered honesty gate: the discretization bias, measured against a grid four times finer, must stay below half the smallest target accuracy. The gate passed with seven factor qubits and five idiosyncratic qubits for the full sample and covid calibrations, bias zero point zero seven to zero point two two basis points, and six and four for the calm calibration. An important consequence of discretizing: the exact answer on the discrete model can be computed classically by direct convolution. For each factor grid point, the portfolio loss is a sum of independent discrete idiosyncratic variables, so the code convolves their distributions one asset at a time on a loss grid of two to the fourteen bins, then mixes over factor points. This exact distribution is the referee for everything downstream: quantum and classical are asked the identical question about the identical object.

Layer three, the classical baseline, which is measured, not assumed. Twenty independent replications of two hundred thousand vectorized Monte Carlo samples each; the spread of the twenty answers estimates the estimator's standard error at that sample count; then the standard one over square root n law scales up to any target: samples needed equals the base count times the square of, base standard error over target error. Multiply by the measured per sample time and you have core seconds per accuracy level. At two basis points, that comes to zero point four core seconds for the full calibration. Hold that number.

Layer four, the oracle, meaning the actual quantum circuit, block by block. Block one: state loaders, one per register, which turn the grid probabilities into amplitudes; implemented with the uniformly controlled rotation decomposition, costing two to the m minus two elementary rotations per m qubit register. Block two: a weighted adder, fixed point arithmetic that accumulates each asset's contribution into a loss register; in the ripple carry construction each input bit controls the addition of a constant into an n sum bit accumulator, costing about two Toffoli gates per accumulator bit per input bit, where a Toffoli is the three qubit gate that is the workhorse of quantum arithmetic. Block three: a comparator against the value at risk threshold, about two Toffoli per accumulator bit, compute and uncompute. Block four, for expected shortfall: a payoff rotation that writes the excess loss onto the marker qubit's amplitude. Around all of it, the Grover iterate: two applications of the whole state preparation, plus a reflection costing about two Toffoli per remaining qubit. One more conversion matters: the target accuracy is in basis points of portfolio return, but the algorithm estimates an amplitude. For expected shortfall at level ninety nine percent, the estimate is value at risk plus the payoff scale c times the amplitude divided by one percent, so the required amplitude error is the target error times one percent divided by c. That conversion, epsilon a equals epsilon times one minus q over c, drives everything, because c grows when tails fatten.

Verification gate one, the end to end run. A truncated two asset instance sized to eighteen qubits was built with the exact blocks above: the state preparation compiles to one thousand fifty two elementary gates, one Grover iterate to five thousand five hundred nine. The exact tail amplitude of this circuit, by brute force enumeration, is zero point one three zero seven three seven. Then I Q A E was run against it at one hundred twenty eight shots per round, across five target accuracies from two times ten to the minus two down to ten to the minus three. Every estimate landed inside its confidence interval, with realized errors between nine times ten to the minus five and five times ten to the minus three. And the headline: fitting cost against accuracy on log log axes gives a slope of minus one point zero three for I Q A E oracle calls versus minus two point zero zero for classical Monte Carlo samples on the same model. Theory says minus one and minus two; measurement says one point zero three and two point zero zero. At accuracy ten to the minus three, I Q A E used nineteen thousand two hundred oracle calls where Monte Carlo needed one hundred thirteen thousand six hundred forty five samples, six times fewer. The quadratic speedup is not an assumption anywhere in this paper; it is a measurement, on a data calibrated circuit. Two implementation war stories are documented as pre result amendments: the originally registered instance width implied a thirty three qubit simulation, one hundred thirty one gigabytes of state vector, because adder carry qubits had been miscounted, so the truncation was reduced twice before any result existed; and the simulator initially rejected the circuit's opaque Grover powers, requiring a transpilation wrapper. Both amendments are logged in the code.

Now the full scale resource count, done two independent ways as a cross check. Method A is analytic: rotations from the loader formula; Toffoli counts from the adder and comparator formulas; each rotation synthesized fault tolerantly at a cost of three times log base two of one over the synthesis accuracy in T gates, where the T gate is the expensive operation error corrected machines must manufacture in special factories, and each Toffoli costs four T. The number of oracle calls comes from the published iterative Q A E worst case bound: one point four over the amplitude error, times a slowly growing logarithmic factor. Method B is constructive: actually build the full scale adder and comparator in the circuit library, decompose to elementary gates, and count. The two methods disagreed by twenty five to twenty six percent, just missing the pre registered twenty percent gate; the cause was identified, the library's generic controlled ripple decomposition is fatter than the literature construction, and the smaller analytic count was used, which favors quantum and cannot rescue the verdict. Totals for the headline point, ninety nine percent expected shortfall to two basis points, full calibration: four point two times ten to the five oracle calls, one point zero times ten to the eleven T gates, two hundred eighty two logical qubits, with seventy four percent of all T gates coming from synthesizing loader rotations and twenty six percent from arithmetic.

Mapping to hardware uses the standard surface code model, and here are its three formulas. Logical error rate per operation: zero point one times, the physical error rate over one percent, raised to the power of, code distance plus one, over two; the code distance d is chosen as the smallest odd number keeping the whole computation's error budget under one percent. Physical qubits: number of logical qubits times two d squared, plus fifteen times two d squared per T factory, the module that manufactures T gates. Wall clock: total T gates times code distance times cycle time, divided by the number of factories, the so called T starved pipeline. At the optimistic corner, physical error ten to the minus four, hundred nanosecond cycles, one hundred factories, the two basis point job needs about eight hundred thousand physical qubits and runs zero point four three hours. The paper notes, deadpan, that the machine itself is surprisingly plausible; hardware roadmaps talk about exactly this scale for the twenty thirties.

So Q A E wins? No, and this is the most instructive number in the program. The same estimate cost zero point four measured core seconds classically, eight milliseconds on a fifty times parallel graphics card. Classical wins by about five orders of magnitude, and the pattern is calibration independent: calm twenty nineteen, zero point one nine hours quantum versus zero point one six seconds classical; covid, zero point nine six hours versus two point eight seconds. The covid calibration is a nice internal check of the regime logic: fat tails raise the classical cost seven fold and simultaneously inflate the payoff scale c, which raises quantum oracle calls two fold; the ratio barely moves. Regime is not a lever here. The frontier figure plots both costs against accuracy on log axes: the classical line is twice as steep, slope minus two against minus one, so the curves do cross, and setting the two cost formulas equal gives the crossover accuracy: epsilon star equals sigma times the classical per sample cost, divided by kappa times the quantum per call cost, with sigma the loss distribution's spread and kappa the I Q A E constant. Plugging in the measured per call to per sample cost ratio, ten to the eighth to ten to the ninth, gives epsilon star near ten to the minus nine, five orders of magnitude beyond any economically meaningful precision. That equation is the paper's single most exportable artifact: every quantity in it is measurable today for any proposed estimation workload, before a dollar of hardware spend.

The criterion also says exactly where to look for a winnable workload: make the classical per sample cost enormous. That is nested simulation, where valuing one outer scenario requires an entire inner Monte Carlo, as in X V A, the derivative valuation adjustments that banks compute across whole books. The program ran three nested cells, and their arc is instructive. Cell one established the refined criterion: the workload multiplier M, the classical cost of one outer scenario relative to plain vectorized arithmetic, must exceed ten to the five, and simultaneously the inner problem must be statistically hard. Cell two built a book of twenty barrier and Asian basket options on the forty nine industries, measured with a validated pricing engine, inner engine checked against a closed form solution to within two standard errors, and found: four point seven microseconds per path, but only about seven thousand inner paths needed to price the book to the frozen tolerance of a tenth of a percent of forward value, giving a per scenario cost of thirty three milliseconds and a multiplier M of twenty two thousand, short of the hundred thousand gate. The culprit: pooled European baskets are low variance targets, statistically easy inside, and an absolute tolerance trivializes exactly the cheap knock in options that were supposed to create difficulty; the measured inner path count was as low as one hundred ninety six. Cell three flipped one convention, making the inner tolerance relative to each option's premium, and got the opposite pathology: one deep out of the money option with a premium around two times ten to the minus six forced four and a half billion inner paths, three hundred thousand core years classically and one hundred fifty million hours even for the quantum machine. Same book, two defensible conventions, two absurd opposite verdicts. The authors stop there, explicitly refusing to shop a third convention, and extract the real lesson, a two level criterion: nested Q A E advantage requires the multiplier M at least ten to the five AND an inner problem needing far more paths than roughly sixteen over the inner amplitude tolerance, because the coherent inner estimation subroutine costs about four times two to the inner register width in overheads, which in cell two came to sixteen thousand inner Grover steps against an inner problem solvable with seven thousand classical paths: when the inner problem is easy, the inner quadratic speedup buys nothing and the nesting overhead eats the multiplier. Books that might clear both bars, path dependent exotics on a hundred thousand paths per scenario, long dated Bermudan books, stochastic volatility capital models, are named as the follow up, with the quantitative bar now fixed in advance.

Verdict, case study two, as frozen: the speedup is real, observed end to end at slope minus one point zero three against minus two point zero zero; the machine is plausible at under a million physical qubits; and standard tail risk workloads are about ten to the five times too cheap per scenario for any of it to matter, with the crossover at a precision nobody prices. Any viable Q A E application must first demonstrate expensive, statistically hard inner valuation under a tolerance schedule committed before measurement. Case study three now moves from estimating numbers to sampling configurations, and produces the strongest quantum favorable measurement of the whole program.
